# Blood Vessels and Circulation

**Three title options:**

1. The Architecture That Moves Blood: Why Vessels Change Shape as Pressure Falls
2. Inside the Pipeline: How Arteries, Capillaries, and Veins Work at Their Limits
3. The Pressure Problem: How Bodies Keep Fluid Moving When Gravity Works Against Them

---

**TL;DR:** Blood moves through the body not in one pipe but in a series of vessels, each with a different wall structure that matches its job. Arteries handle high pressure close to the heart; capillaries slow the blood to let exchange happen; veins collect what's left and return it against gravity. The mechanics turn out to be simple once you see why each vessel is built the way it is.

---

## 1. Chapter Opening: The Puzzle of Pressure

You feel your pulse in your wrist. Press your finger to your neck. That rhythmic surge tells you something is happening that your eyes cannot see. Blood is not flowing smoothly through your vessels. It is surging—pushing hard against the artery walls as your heart contracts, then falling away as your heart relaxes. The surge happens 70 times a minute, 100,000 times a day, a hundred million times in a lifetime. Your body must keep this happening or you die.

But here is the puzzle: the surge starts at high pressure in the arteries near your heart. By the time blood reaches your fingertips or your toes, the pressure has fallen to nearly nothing. By the time it reaches your capillaries—the tiny vessels where actual exchange with your cells occurs—the blood is moving slowly, almost lazily. Yet somehow blood still gets back to your heart against gravity. When you stand up, all the blood in your legs does not pool there and stay. It returns. Why?

The answer is not one answer. It is written into the structure of the vessels themselves. Arteries are thick-walled and muscular. Capillaries are one cell thick. Veins are thin but enormous, built less like pipes and more like deflatable storage bags. Each vessel is built for the job it has to do. Once you see the structure, the physics almost explains itself.

## 2. Concept 1: The Wall Structure That Matches the Pressure

### Cold Open

Slice an artery in cross-section under a microscope. Three distinct layers appear, concentric rings like a tree trunk. The innermost layer is simple squamous epithelium—the endothelium, a single sheet of cells flat against the vessel wall. Outside that, a thick middle layer of smooth muscle. Outside that, a sheath of connective tissue. Now slice a vein the same way. You see the same three layers, but their proportions are inverted. The vein's middle layer is thin. The outer layer is thick. The artery looks like a tightly wound spring. The vein looks like a deflated balloon. The difference is not decoration. It is the difference between a high-pressure system and a low-pressure system, written in tissue.

### Mechanism: The Three Tunics

All blood vessels except capillaries have three layers, called tunics (from the Latin tunica, a garment). The architecture shifts as pressure falls. This is the key.

**Tunica intima (the inner layer).** This is the endothelium—the lining where blood actually touches. It is permeable. It is also active. Recent work has shown it is not merely a passive barrier but an organ in itself, releasing chemicals called endothelins that can constrict smooth muscle and change local blood pressure. Damage to this lining—from inflammation, high blood glucose, turbulent flow—exposes collagen fibers beneath, which trigger clot formation. The tunica intima also contains the basement membrane (basal lamina), a meshwork of proteins that binds the endothelium to the connective tissue beneath and permits material to pass through.

**Tunica media (the middle layer).** In arteries, this is the thickest layer. It consists of smooth muscle cells arranged in circular sheets, supported by elastic fibers running in spiral patterns. Contraction of these circular muscles decreases the vessel diameter—vasoconstriction. Relaxation increases diameter—vasodilation. The effect is dramatic. A small change in diameter changes resistance to flow by the fourth power (we will see this in the Poiseuille equation shortly). In veins, the tunica media is much thinner and contains more collagen fibers, fewer elastic fibers. Veins lack the muscular strength to contract forcefully.

**Tunica externa (the outer layer).** In arteries close to the heart, this is thin. In veins, it is thick. In larger vessels, it contains small blood vessels within its own wall—the vasa vasorum, "vessels of the vessel"—to feed the endothelial cells and smooth muscle. Because arterial pressure is high, these nutritive vessels must run in the outer layers; if they ran near the lumen, the pressure would collapse them. Veins have lower pressure, so vasa vasorum can run closer to the lumen.

### Trade-off: Strength vs. Compliance

An artery near the heart must withstand the surge of blood ejected by ventricular contraction. Its walls are thick. Thick walls cost energy—the smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts that build them need oxygen and nutrients. But thick walls buy compliance—the ability to expand when blood surges, then recoil. This elastic recoil helps maintain the pressure gradient that drives blood forward. The elastic fibers are so prominent in arteries near the heart (elastic arteries) that they appear wavy under the microscope.

Farther from the heart, where the surge has dampened, arteries transition. Elastic fibers decrease. Smooth muscle increases. These muscular arteries are built for control, not compliance. They can vasoconstrict and vasodilate to regulate blood flow to tissues. A vein has no compliance requirement—pressure is already low. Its thin walls stretch easily. This is not weakness; it is adaptation. Veins are capacitance vessels, holding 64 percent of the body's blood volume at any given moment. When the body needs blood returned quickly to the heart—during exercise, during hemorrhage—the sympathetic nervous system signals venoconstriction. The thin walls stiffen slightly, pressure increases, and blood flows back faster. The mechanism is gentle but effective.

### Worked Example: Why Does Artery Wall Thickness Correlate with Distance from the Heart?

Assume the aorta (the main artery leaving the heart) ejects blood at 120 mm Hg systolic, 80 mm Hg diastolic. The pulse—the difference—is 40 mm Hg. The aorta's wall is thick, with abundant elastic fibers. By the time blood reaches arterioles at the periphery, systolic pressure has fallen to roughly 50 mm Hg, diastolic to 30 mm Hg. The pulse is 20 mm Hg. Arterioles have much thinner walls. By the capillaries, pressure is around 35 mm Hg at the arterial end, falling to 18 mm Hg at the venous end. The wall is one cell thick. The drop is continuous and predictable. Why? Because as pressure falls, the wall does not need to withstand as much stress. Compliance becomes less critical. Control of local flow becomes more important. The tunica media thins; arterioles become resistance vessels, not conductance vessels. The architecture scales to the physics.

### Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1:** "Veins are weak vessels that easily rupture." 

False. Veins are not weak; they are adapted to low pressure. You can damage a vein—deep vein thrombosis, varicose veins—but the damage comes from pooling blood, bacterial infection, or valve failure, not from structural weakness. Veins can withstand their normal operating pressure indefinitely.

**Misconception 2:** "The three layers are the same in every vessel."

False. The proportions shift dramatically. In arteries, tunica media is thickest. In veins, tunica externa is thickest. In capillaries, only the tunica intima exists—the endothelium and its basement membrane, nothing more. This is not variability; it is systematic adaptation.

**Misconception 3:** "Elastic fibers are found equally throughout the arterial tree."

False. Elastic fibers are concentrated in large arteries near the heart. Muscular arteries have less. Arterioles have almost none. Each transition reflects the physics. Near the heart, you need compliance to absorb the surge. At the periphery, you need smooth muscle for control.

## 3. Concept 2: The Physics of Flow—Why Radius Dominates Everything

### Cold Open

A physician prescribes a drug that causes a small artery to constrict—to shrink its diameter by half. You might expect blood flow through that artery to fall by half. It does not. Flow falls to one-sixteenth of what it was. Conversely, a drug that causes vasodilation—doubling the diameter—increases flow sixteen-fold. This is not an exaggeration; it is the mathematics. A small change in radius cascades into an enormous change in flow. This is the principle that makes arterioles the site of greatest resistance in the entire vascular system and the primary point of blood pressure control.

### Mechanism: Poiseuille's Equation

The French physician Jean Louis Marie Poiseuille derived an equation in the 1840s that describes how fluids flow through tubes. The equation applies to blood:

$$\text{Blood flow} = \frac{\pi \Delta P \, r^4}{8 \eta \lambda}$$

Where:
- $\Delta P$ is the pressure difference driving flow
- $r$ is the radius of the vessel
- $\eta$ (eta) is blood viscosity (thickness)
- $\lambda$ (lambda) is vessel length

Three variables determine resistance. Rearranging:

$$\text{Resistance} = \frac{8 \eta \lambda}{\pi r^4}$$

Notice: radius is raised to the fourth power. Viscosity and length change slowly in your body. Radius changes moment to moment. A slight contraction of smooth muscle reduces $r$. Since $r^4$ is in the denominator, resistance skyrockets.

Example: If an arteriole constricts to half its original radius, resistance increases by a factor of $2^4 = 16$. If it dilates to twice its original radius, resistance falls to $1/16$ of what it was, and flow increases sixteen-fold.

This is why arterioles are called resistance vessels. They have small lumens to begin with. A small arteriole has a diameter of roughly 30 micrometers. Capillaries are even smaller—5 to 10 micrometers. Yet capillaries are not the site of greatest resistance. Why? Because there are vastly more capillaries than arterioles. When many small vessels run in parallel, total cross-sectional area increases. The total resistance of the capillary bed is actually lower than the resistance of the arterioles that feed them. Resistance is distributed across the network. The arterioles concentrate it in a single checkpoint.

### Trade-off: Regulation vs. Flow

High resistance at the arterioles means tight control. The sympathetic nervous system can cause arteriolar vasoconstriction to raise blood pressure or cause vasodilation to lower it. This is why blood pressure is regulated primarily by arteriolar diameter, not by the diameter of larger arteries. Large arteries are conductance vessels—they accept blood from the heart and hand it off. Arterioles are control valves.

But there is a cost. Resistance means energy dissipation. Blood moving through arterioles loses kinetic energy to friction. This is why blood velocity (the actual speed of blood movement) falls dramatically as blood moves from arteries to arterioles to capillaries. Slow velocity in capillaries is a feature, not a bug. The slowness gives time for exchange—oxygen and nutrients diffuse out; carbon dioxide and wastes diffuse in. The price is paid in resistance and pressure drop.

### Worked Example: Calculating the Effect of a Blood Vessel Constricting

Assume a muscular artery has a radius of 1 mm and blood pressure across it drops from 80 mm Hg to 60 mm Hg. Flow is proportional to $\Delta P / R$, or $(80-60) / R_1$. Now the vessel constricts to 0.8 mm radius (a 20 percent reduction). Resistance changes by a factor of $(1/0.8)^4 = (1.25)^4 = 2.44$. The new resistance is $2.44 R_1$. If pressure drop remains 20 mm Hg, new flow is $(20) / (2.44 R_1) = 0.41$ of the original. Flow falls by roughly 60 percent from a 20 percent reduction in radius. This is why even slight narrowing from atherosclerosis causes ischemia—tissue underperfusion—when the blockage reaches the critical arterioles.

### Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1:** "Blood pressure is maintained primarily by the elasticity of large arteries."

Partially true, but incomplete. Elastic recoil of large arteries does help maintain diastolic pressure between heartbeats. But blood pressure regulation—the active control of pressure from moment to moment—is dominated by arteriolar vasoconstriction and vasodilation. Large arteries are too stiff and too proximal to allow rapid control. Arterioles are the control point.

**Misconception 2:** "Blood viscosity is constant and unchanging."

Usually true, but not always. Viscosity depends on hematocrit (the fraction of blood that is red blood cells) and plasma protein levels. In anemia, blood viscosity decreases, and flow increases—a compensatory mechanism. In polycythemia (excess red blood cells), viscosity increases, and flow decreases despite higher oxygen carrying capacity. The kidney produces erythropoietin to maintain a balance.

**Misconception 3:** "Capillaries have the greatest resistance because they are the smallest."

False. Capillaries are smallest individually, but there are so many in parallel that total cross-sectional area is enormous. Arterioles are where resistance concentrates. This is a key principle: resistance in series adds; resistance in parallel decreases.

## 4. Concept 3: Capillary Exchange—The Starling Forces

### Cold Open

You stand at the edge of a pier. Fish swim below the surface. Light bends at the water-air boundary. The deeper you look, the hazier it becomes. At the capillary bed, you face a similar boundary—between blood plasma and tissue fluid. Plasma proteins remain inside. Water and small molecules move back and forth, driven by two opposing forces. The pressure of blood pushes water out. The osmotic pull of plasma proteins draws water back in. The balance is precarious. Get it wrong, and fluid accumulates in tissues—edema. This exchange is where the cardiovascular system actually does its work. Everything upstream exists to make this happen.

### Mechanism: The Starling Forces

Two pressures drive capillary exchange. The interaction is the Starling principle, named after Ernest Starling.

**Capillary hydrostatic pressure (CHP)** is the blood pressure inside the capillary. At the arterial end of a capillary bed, CHP is roughly 35 mm Hg. As blood moves through the capillary and loses fluid, CHP falls to roughly 18 mm Hg at the venous end. CHP pushes fluid out.

**Blood colloidal osmotic pressure (BCOP)** is the osmotic pull exerted by plasma proteins suspended in blood. Plasma proteins—albumin, globulins, fibrinogen—are too large to cross the capillary wall. They remain in the plasma. Their presence creates a concentration gradient. Water, following osmosis, is drawn toward higher solute concentration. The BCOP is roughly 25 mm Hg throughout the capillary bed because proteins do not leave; only water moves. BCOP draws fluid in.

**Interstitial fluid pressures** (tissue-side pressures) are negligible in healthy tissue. The hydrostatic pressure in tissue fluid is near zero. The osmotic pressure of tissue fluid (due to the small number of proteins that have leaked out) is near zero. So we ignore them.

**Net filtration pressure (NFP)** at any point in the capillary is the difference between CHP and BCOP:

$$\text{NFP} = \text{CHP} - \text{BCOP}$$

At the arterial end: $\text{NFP} = 35 - 25 = 10$ mm Hg. Filtration dominates. Fluid moves out.

At the midpoint: $\text{NFP} = 25 - 25 = 0$ mm Hg. No net movement.

At the venous end: $\text{NFP} = 18 - 25 = -7$ mm Hg. Reabsorption dominates. Fluid moves in.

### Quantification: The Daily Fluid Flux

This matters because the numbers are large. About 24 liters of fluid per day filter out of capillary beds into tissue spaces. Only 20.4 liters are reabsorbed by capillaries. The difference—roughly 3.6 liters per day—is picked up by lymphatic capillaries, which are even more permeable than blood capillaries. Lymph is recycled blood plasma. If lymphatic drainage fails (tumor growth blocking lymph vessels, surgical removal of lymph nodes), fluid accumulates—lymphedema. If reabsorption fails because plasma protein levels drop (malnutrition, liver disease), edema develops. The system is quantitative and fragile.

### Trade-off: Exchange Surface vs. Flow Speed

Capillaries are built for exchange, not flow. They are 5 to 10 micrometers in diameter. Red blood cells are 7 to 8 micrometers. Individual cells squeeze through, deforming slightly. Blood moves slowly. The slowness is intentional. Slow movement gives time for diffusion. Oxygen, dissolved in plasma, diffuses from capillary into tissue. Carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction. Glucose and amino acids move across via transporters or through intercellular clefts (gaps between endothelial cells). Larger molecules move through fenestrations (pores) in capillary walls—particularly in organs where exchange is intense (kidneys, liver, endocrine glands).

But there are three types of capillaries, and they differ in their leakiness.

**Continuous capillaries** (the most common) have tight junctions between endothelial cells but leave intercellular clefts open. Water and small molecules pass through the clefts. Capillaries in the brain are an exception—they are part of the blood-brain barrier. Their tight junctions are truly tight, and they lack intercellular clefts. The barrier keeps pathogens and toxins out of the brain.

**Fenestrated capillaries** have pores (fenestrations) in the endothelial cells themselves. These allow larger molecules to pass. Fenestrated capillaries are found in the kidneys (where filtration is the primary function), the small intestine (where nutrient absorption is extensive), and endocrine glands (where hormones must pass from secretory cells into blood).

**Sinusoid capillaries** are the leakiest. They look like Swiss cheese—large intercellular gaps, incomplete basement membrane, open fenestrations. Blood flow is slow. Exchange of large molecules, even cells, is extensive. Sinusoids are found in the liver (processing materials from the digestive tract), bone marrow (where new blood cells enter circulation), and spleen (where old blood cells are removed).

The leakiness is not random. It is matched to tissue need. In tissues where exchange must be slow and selective (brain, most muscle), capillaries are tight. In tissues where exchange must be rapid and massive (kidney, liver), capillaries are open.

### Worked Example: Calculating Net Filtration Pressure and Predicting Fluid Fate

A patient has normal CHP (35 mm Hg at arterial end) but low BCOP (15 mm Hg) due to liver disease causing low plasma protein levels.

At arterial end: $\text{NFP} = 35 - 15 = 20$ mm Hg. Filtration is doubled.

At venous end (assume CHP has fallen to 18 mm Hg): $\text{NFP} = 18 - 15 = 3$ mm Hg. Reabsorption is reduced.

The balance tips. More fluid filters out than is reabsorbed. Edema develops. The amount of edema depends on which tissues are affected and how much extra fluid accumulates. In the legs, gravity makes it worse—blood pools, CHP at capillaries increases further, filtration increases more. This is why liver disease patients with low protein develop ankle edema and swelling.

### Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1:** "Plasma proteins leak constantly from capillaries, so their concentration in tissue fluid should equal their concentration in blood."

False. Small amounts do leak, but the concentration in tissue fluid remains much lower (roughly 2 g/dL in tissue fluid vs. 7 g/dL in plasma). Lymphatic drainage removes excess protein. The gradient is maintained.

**Misconception 2:** "Capillary exchange is the same throughout the body."

False. Exchange varies by capillary type and tissue need. Fenestrated capillaries exchange more. Continuous capillaries in the brain exchange almost nothing (blood-brain barrier). Sinusoids exchange massively. The type of capillary is matched to tissue function.

**Misconception 3:** "Edema always means too much fluid intake or kidney failure."

False. Edema can result from: low plasma protein (malnutrition, liver disease), high capillary pressure (heart failure, venous obstruction), lymphatic failure (tumor, surgery), or increased capillary permeability (inflammation, allergic reaction). Each cause requires different treatment.

## 5. Integration: How the Pieces Work Together

The three concepts fit into one system. The heart pumps blood into elastic arteries, which expand and recoil to smooth the surge. Muscular arteries branch and distribute blood. Arterioles constrict and dilate to control local pressure and flow—this is where blood pressure is regulated. Blood enters capillaries slowly. Exchange happens over the length of the capillary. Venules collect blood. Veins return it to the heart.

The entire system is maintained by a pressure gradient. Pressure starts high near the heart and falls continuously. Pressure drives flow according to Poiseuille's equation. When resistance is high (arterioles constricted), pressure drop is steep. When resistance is low (capillary bed in parallel), pressure drop is gentle. Blood velocity—the actual speed—falls dramatically as resistance increases. Slow velocity in capillaries enables exchange.

Capillary exchange depends on the balance of Starling forces. The balance depends on all three variables: capillary hydrostatic pressure (determined by arteriolar resistance upstream), plasma protein concentration (determined by nutrition and liver function), and lymphatic drainage (determined by the health of the lymphatic system). Break any one, and fluid accumulates.

The system is regulated by two mechanisms. The nervous system controls arteriolar diameter via sympathetic innervation. The endocrine system controls blood volume and osmolality via hormones (aldosterone, ADH). Both adjust resistance and pressure to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) roughly between 70 and 110 mm Hg. Below 60 mm Hg for extended periods, tissues become hypoxic. Above 140 mm Hg chronically, vessel walls are damaged and arteriosclerosis progresses.

### Warm-up

1. **Identify the layers.** Look at a cross-section of an artery. Name the three tunics and describe what each one is made of.

2. **Explain vasodilation.** When smooth muscle in the tunica media of an arteriole relaxes, what happens to the lumen diameter? What happens to resistance? What happens to blood flow through that vessel?

3. **State the Starling principle.** At the arterial end of a capillary, CHP is 35 mm Hg and BCOP is 25 mm Hg. Is fluid moving into or out of the capillary?

### Application

4. **Interpret Poiseuille.** A patient has atherosclerosis narrowing a coronary artery. The radius of the narrowed section is 60 percent of normal. By what factor does resistance increase in this segment? Assume pressure drop across the segment is the same before and after narrowing. What happens to flow?

5. **Troubleshoot edema.** A patient with cirrhosis of the liver develops ankle swelling. Explain why low plasma protein levels lead to edema using the Starling forces. Which pressure changes? How does the change affect net filtration pressure?

6. **Integrate the systems.** During intense exercise, blood is diverted to skeletal muscle. Describe the changes that occur in: (a) arteriolar diameter in muscle, (b) arteriolar diameter in the skin, (c) blood flow to muscle, (d) blood flow to the brain (which does not change), and explain how these changes are regulated.

### Synthesis

7. **Explain a pathology.** Describe what happens when a blood clot (thrombus) forms in a deep leg vein. How do the structural differences between arteries and veins make a venous thrombosis more likely than an arterial thrombosis in the leg?

8. **Predict consequences.** A patient takes a drug that causes generalized vasodilation in arterioles. What happens to: systemic vascular resistance, mean arterial pressure, arteriolar blood pressure, capillary hydrostatic pressure, net filtration pressure at capillary beds. Which tissue is at highest risk for edema?

### Challenge

9. **Quantitative reasoning.** A patient's capillaries have a capillary hydrostatic pressure of 32 mm Hg at the arterial end and blood colloidal osmotic pressure of 25 mm Hg. Tissue pressure is negligible. Calculate net filtration pressure at the arterial end. Assume CHP falls by 14 mm Hg across the capillary. Calculate net filtration pressure at the venous end. Is this patient likely to develop edema? Justify.

10. **Integrate mechanisms.** The baroreceptor reflex is a rapid neural mechanism that stabilizes blood pressure. When blood pressure rises, baroreceptors in the carotid sinus signal the brain. The brain increases parasympathetic tone and decreases sympathetic tone. Using what you know about arteriolar control and cardiac regulation, explain how these neural changes lower blood pressure back toward normal.

## 7. Chapter Summary

Blood vessels are not uniform pipes. They change structure systematically as pressure falls. Arteries, close to the heart and exposed to high pressure, have thick muscular walls with elastic fibers. Arterioles are the resistance vessels where blood pressure is actively controlled. Capillaries are one cell thick, optimized for exchange, not flow. Veins are thin-walled and compliant, serving as low-pressure return vessels and blood reservoirs.

The physics of blood flow is governed by Poiseuille's equation. Resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of vessel radius. This means small changes in arteriolar diameter cause enormous changes in resistance and flow. This property makes arterioles the control point for blood pressure regulation.

Capillary exchange is driven by the balance of hydrostatic and osmotic pressures—the Starling forces. Capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out. Blood colloidal osmotic pressure pulls fluid back in. The balance shifts as blood moves through the capillary. At the arterial end, filtration dominates. At the venous end, reabsorption dominates. Lymphatic vessels recover the excess fluid filtered out. When the balance fails—from low plasma protein, high capillary pressure, or lymphatic obstruction—edema results.

The system is integrated and regulated. Sympathetic nerves control arteriolar diameter. Endocrine hormones control blood volume. Together they maintain mean arterial pressure within a narrow range, ensuring adequate tissue perfusion while preventing vessel damage. The entire system scales its structure to its function—compliance near the heart, control at the arterioles, exchange at the capillaries, return through the veins.

## 8. Connections Forward

This chapter explains the structure that makes circulation possible. The next chapters will examine what happens when this structure fails. Hypertension develops when resistance is chronically high—when arterioles fail to dilate or when arteries stiffen. Atherosclerosis begins when the endothelium is damaged and heals with scar tissue, reducing compliance and increasing resistance further. Heart failure occurs when the heart can no longer generate enough pressure to overcome resistance and maintain adequate cardiac output. Edema and varicose veins result when the Starling balance tips toward filtration. Understanding the mechanics of normal circulation—how pressure falls systematically, how resistance concentrates at the arterioles, how exchange happens through the balance of forces—makes each of these pathologies intelligible.

## Appendix: What Would Change My Mind

Evidence that plasma proteins contribute to capillary filtration pressure would revise this chapter. The current model assumes plasma proteins do not significantly alter the osmotic gradient across the capillary wall. If new research showed that protein-bound water or protein-protein interactions change the osmotic effect, the quantitative predictions would need adjustment.

## Still Puzzling

Why the blood-brain barrier is impermeable while capillaries elsewhere are freely permeable remains incompletely understood. Astrocyte end-feet (extensions of brain support cells) interact with capillary endothelium, but the molecular mechanism that suppresses intercellular clefts is not fully known.
