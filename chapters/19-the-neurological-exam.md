# The Neurological Exam: Reading Damage by Its Function

**TL;DR:** A ten-minute series of simple tests that ask the body to do ordinary things reveals where in the nervous system something has gone wrong. Damage and disease leave signatures in movement, sensation, and reflex—signatures a clinician can read without imaging.

---

## Chapter opening: A man arrives at the hospital

A man arrives at the emergency department unable to move his left arm. An hour ago he felt a sudden pins-and-needles sensation down his left side, then weakness in his leg. The physicians do not yet know where the stroke has occurred—the brain contains perhaps 86 billion neurons, distributed across structures you would need an atlas to name. But within five minutes, a neurologist puts him through a series of tasks. Raise your eyebrows. Stick out your tongue. Feel this? What about this? Follow my finger. Squeeze my hands. Push down against me.

By the time those five minutes are over, the clinical team has a map. The lesion is not in the motor cortex. It is not in the brainstem. It is in the right internal capsule—a white matter bundle carrying commands from the cortex down toward the spinal cord. Imaging will confirm this. But the imaging is not what pointed them there. A cotton swab and a reflex hammer did.

The neurological exam is systematic localization made visible. Every test is a question: *If this part of the nervous system worked, would you be able to do this?* By asking that question methodically, across five major regions—mind, eyes and face, sensation, movement, and coordination—a clinician converts behavior into anatomy. Deficits become addresses.

---

## Concept 1: Five Tests, Five Regions

The exam is divided into sections, and this division is anatomical. Each section probes a specific region of the CNS and reveals what that region controls.

**The mental status exam** tests the highest functions: memory, language, orientation, judgment. These depend on the cerebral cortex—the thin wrinkled surface of the brain where consciousness lives. You ask a patient their name, the date, who is the president. You ask them to remember three words and recall them five minutes later. You ask them to interpret a proverb or generate words that start with a given letter. When these fail, you know something is wrong in the cortex.

**The cranial nerve exam** tests the twelve nerves that serve the head and neck and the brainstem structures they connect to. Three test smell, vision, hearing. Three control the muscles that move the eyes. Four control the muscles of the face, jaw, throat, and tongue. One controls the neck. The exam is a tour through the brainstem—a journey that moves from the midbrain through the pons and down to the medulla. When the patient cannot feel the right side of their face, the trigeminal nerve is implicated. When their eyes won't move together, the oculomotor complex is involved.

**The sensory exam** tests the pathways of the spinal cord that carry information from the body back to the brain. Light touch. Pain. Vibration. Proprioception—the sense of where your limbs are in space. These different sensory qualities travel in different pathways. Fine touch travels in the dorsal columns, ascending on the same side before crossing in the medulla. Pain crosses immediately upon entry into the spinal cord. This segregation means sensory deficits can tell you not just *where* the lesion is, but *how the spinal cord is divided*.

**The motor exam** tests the corticospinal tract—the pathway from the motor cortex down through the brainstem and spinal cord to the muscles. You watch for muscle tone. You test strength. You look for asymmetry. A patient whose left arm drifts downward when held out straight is showing you a lesion on the right side of the brain.

**The coordination exam** tests the cerebellum, which compares motor commands with sensory feedback to make movement smooth and accurate. The patient touches their finger to their nose, then to the examiner's finger, back and forth. The patient walks in a straight line, or stands on one foot, or walks heel-to-toe. The cerebellum is a comparator—it does not initiate movement, but it corrects it in real time. When it fails, movement becomes clumsy and uncoordinated.

These five sections are not independent. A patient who cannot remember three words might also be unable to follow a command because language itself is damaged. A patient whose arm is weak might stumble during a gait test because motor weakness affects balance. But each section illuminates a different level of the nervous system, and together they create a map of where something has gone wrong.

## Concept 2: The Brainstem Tour—Reading Cranial Nerve Deficits as Localization

The twelve cranial nerves emerge from the brainstem. Unlike spinal nerves, which are relatively uniform, the cranial nerves have wildly different functions. The olfactory nerve carries smell. The oculomotor, trochlear, and abducens nerves move the eyes. The trigeminal nerve carries sensation from the face and controls chewing. The facial nerve controls the muscles of expression and carries taste from the front two-thirds of the tongue. The vagus nerve, the long wanderer, controls swallowing and the organs of the chest and belly.

The power of the cranial nerve exam is this: damage to any one of these nerves points to a specific location in the brainstem, and sometimes to damage in neighboring structures that have nothing directly to do with that nerve.

Take the vestibulocochlear nerve—the eighth cranial nerve—which carries balance and hearing from the inner ear to the brainstem. It emerges from the medulla carrying two distinct sensory systems that are anatomically neighbors. The vestibular nuclei process balance; the cochlear nuclei process sound. A patient with Ménière's disease might experience vertigo, tinnitus, and hearing loss—all from a disturbance in fluid within the inner ear. But a patient with a stroke in the medulla near these nuclei might lose one sense or both, depending on the size and location of the lesion. A small stroke might affect only the cochlea-related fibers, leaving balance intact. A larger one affects both.

Or consider the hypoglossal nerve—the twelfth cranial nerve—which controls the tongue. A simple test: stick out your tongue. In a healthy patient, it goes straight. In a patient with a lesion on one side of the medulla, the tongue deviates toward the side of damage. This happens because the corticospinal tract—the major motor pathway—has already crossed in the medulla, so damage on the right side of the brain affects the left side of the body. But the hypoglossal nerve has not yet crossed. It controls the ipsilateral muscles. So a right-sided medullary lesion will cause the tongue to point to the right—toward the damaged side of the brainstem.

This is not a coincidence. The hypoglossal nucleus sits near the cardiovascular center of the medulla, near the respiratory nuclei, near the vagal nuclei that regulate digestion. A patient with a medullary stroke large enough to damage the hypoglossal nucleus will likely have damage to these neighboring structures as well. The tongue becomes a window onto damage to the region.

The eye movements offer another window. Three nerves—the oculomotor (III), trochlear (IV), and abducens (VI)—control six muscles that move each eye. These muscles must move together, controlled by nuclei that are far apart: the abducens nucleus in the medulla, the oculomotor nucleus in the midbrain. The medial longitudinal fasciculus—a bundle of white matter running through the brainstem—connects these nuclei and coordinates their activity. When both eyes move smoothly in the same direction while tracking an object, the MLF is working. When one eye fails to move inward while the other moves outward, the MLF is damaged. This is called internuclear ophthalmoplegia. The lesion might be in the MLF itself, a small white matter tract, or in the region around it. A patient with multiple sclerosis—a disease that damages myelin in the CNS—often presents with internuclear ophthalmoplegia because the demyelinated plaques occur in the brainstem.

The gag reflex offers a tour through the brainstem in functional form. The examiner touches the back of the throat with a tongue depressor. Sensory information travels inward along the glossopharyngeal nerve (IX), but the motor response—the contraction of the pharyngeal muscles—is carried by the vagus nerve (X). These two nerves have nuclei in the medulla that are anatomical neighbors. A stroke that damages the nucleus of one often damages the other. The gag reflex becomes a test of medullary integrity.

This is the principle: the cranial nerves are a tour guide through the brainstem. Each nerve emerges from a specific location. Damage at that location creates a signature. The signature might be loss of that nerve's function alone, but often it is not. The neighboring structures—whether nuclei or passing fiber tracts—are affected too. This is how a simple test—"Follow my finger"—can tell you that there is a lesion in the medulla, or the pons, or the midbrain. The brainstem is small, perhaps the width of your thumb. The territory is dense. Nothing sits alone.

## Concept 3: Upper Motor Neuron vs. Lower Motor Neuron—The Reflex as Evidence

Motor control has two stages. The first stage is the upper motor neuron—a cell in the motor cortex whose axon descends through the brainstem and spinal cord as the corticospinal tract. Most of these axons cross in the medulla, in a region called the pyramids. A few do not. These fibers synapse on the second stage, the lower motor neuron—a cell in the ventral horn of the spinal cord whose axon extends to skeletal muscle.

Damage at different levels of this system produces different signatures. This is the distinction that separates upper motor neuron disease from lower motor neuron disease.

A patient with an upper motor neuron lesion—a stroke in the motor cortex or a cut through the pyramids of the medulla—loses the ability to move voluntarily. But the resting tone of the muscle is preserved. The muscles are not floppy. They are tight, contracted, spastic. The deep tendon reflexes—the knee-jerk reflex, the ankle reflex—are exaggerated. If the examiner taps the patellar tendon, the quadriceps contracts vigorously. If the examiner stretches a muscle passively, the patient cannot relax it—the muscle resists and then suddenly releases, like the closing of a pocket knife. This is the clasp-knife response.

A patient with a lower motor neuron lesion—damage to the nerve root, peripheral nerve, or the motor neuron itself in the spinal cord—has the opposite picture. The muscle is completely paralyzed or weakened. But the resting tone is gone. The muscles are flaccid, floppy, atrophied. The deep tendon reflexes are absent or severely diminished. The muscle does not resist passive stretch.

The difference is profound and reveals the mechanism of motor control. The upper motor neuron does not directly contract the muscle. It modulates the lower motor neuron, which maintains a background level of activity. This background activity can be felt as resting muscle tone. When the upper motor neuron is cut, this modulation is lost, but the lower motor neuron still has its own reflexive responses. The reflex arc—sensory input from the muscle to the spinal cord, direct synapse onto the motor neuron, motor output back to the muscle—is still intact. With the higher command gone, the reflexes become exaggerated, running unchecked. The muscle becomes hypercontractile.

When the lower motor neuron is cut, the reflex arc itself is broken. There is no innervation of the muscle at all. The muscle atrophies because it receives no signal. The reflexes disappear because the neuron that would respond to the reflex stimulus is dead.

The Babinski sign is the most famous test of this distinction. In an infant or a patient with upper motor neuron damage, brushing the sole of the foot produces an upward extension of the toes and a fanning out—dorsiflexion of the foot. In a healthy adult, the same stimulus produces a downward curling of the toes—plantar flexion. This change happens as the brain matures and the corticospinal tract develops. The descending corticospinal input modifies the reflex response. In upper motor neuron disease, this descending modulation is lost. The reflex reverts to its infantile form. The presence of a Babinski sign in an adult is a red flag: the corticospinal tract is damaged somewhere.

These tests—the strength test, the tone test, the reflex test, the Babinski sign—are not separate pieces of information. They are a unified picture. Together, they answer the question: *Is the damage above or below the point where the corticospinal tract synapses on the lower motor neuron?* That answer narrows the lesion from an entire hemisphere to a specific level of the spinal cord.

Consider a clinical example: a patient with weakness in the right arm and leg, and an extensor Babinski sign on the right. The examiner already knows the lesion is in the left side of the brain, because the corticospinal tract crosses. But where? If the mental status exam is normal and the cranial nerves are intact, the lesion is not in the cortex or the brainstem. The patient can speak, understand, move their face symmetrically. But the motor tract is damaged. The lesion is probably in the internal capsule, the white matter bundle that carries the corticospinal tract as it descends from the cortex. An imaging study will confirm this. But the neurological exam has already told the story. The exam converted the symptom—weakness—into anatomy.

## Integration: The Exam as a Unifying Logic

The neurological exam is structured this way because the nervous system is structured this way. The brain contains many functions—cognition, sensation, movement, balance, emotion, memory—but these are not distributed randomly. They are localized to specific regions. A lesion in one region produces a specific pattern of loss. The exam is designed to elicit that pattern.

This is why the exam takes only five to ten minutes in the hands of someone trained in it. It is not a comprehensive test of every possible neurological function. It is a systematic survey designed to answer one question quickly: *Where in the nervous system has something gone wrong?* In the emergency department, this question is urgent. A stroke patient whose tissue is dying has a few hours—possibly only two or three—during which thrombolytic therapy might restore blood flow and save brain tissue. The neurological exam does not diagnose the stroke itself. Imaging does that. But the exam tells the team where to look and how serious the damage might be. It is the first step in triage.

In the classroom, the exam serves a different purpose. It is a way to see the nervous system in action, to move from anatomy—the names of structures—to function. The cranial nerves are not just twelve items to memorize. They are a functional tour through the brainstem, and understanding the tour means understanding the brainstem's geography. The sensory exam is not just a test of touch and pain. It is a revelation of the segregation of function within the spinal cord. The reflex exam is not just a measure of reflexive response. It is evidence of the two-stage motor system and how lesions at different levels produce different patterns.

This integration of structure and function is what makes the neurological exam powerful. You cannot fully understand the exam without understanding the anatomy. You cannot fully understand the anatomy without working through the exam. The exam is, in that sense, an instrument that makes the nervous system visible through behavior.

## Five Graduated Exercises

### Warm-up

1. Draw a diagram of the spinal cord in cross-section. Label the dorsal and ventral columns of white matter. In the dorsal column, trace the path of a light-touch stimulus from the fingertip to the brain, noting where the fiber crosses. In the lateral column, trace the path of a descending motor command from the motor cortex to the muscles of the leg, noting where those fibers cross. What would happen to sensation and movement if only the right half of the spinal cord were damaged? Which sensations would be lost on which side of the body?

2. List the twelve cranial nerves in order. Next to each, write one or two words that capture its primary function (smell, vision, eye movement, facial muscles, taste, hearing, neck movement, etc.). Then, without looking at your list, close your eyes and picture the brainstem from below. Where does the olfactory nerve emerge? Where does the vagus nerve emerge? Which nerves exit from the midbrain, which from the pons, which from the medulla?

### Application

3. A patient presents with weakness in the right arm and leg, an extensor Babinski sign on the right, and a tongue that deviates to the left when protruded. The mental status exam is normal. The cranial nerves are intact except for the hypoglossal nerve. Where is the lesion? Why does the tongue deviate to the left when the motor weakness is on the right? Sketch the level of the brainstem involved and explain which tract or nucleus is damaged.

4. A patient reports sudden loss of hearing in the left ear accompanied by vertigo (dizziness) and nausea. The examiner performs the Weber test with a tuning fork: the sound lateralizes to the right ear. Perform the Rinne test on the left ear and predict the result. Is this damage to the vestibulocochlear nerve, the inner ear, or the brainstem? Explain the reasoning.

### Synthesis

5. You are a clinician in an emergency department. A 62-year-old patient arrives with sudden weakness in the left arm and left leg. You have five minutes to perform a rapid neurological exam before imaging. Design the sequence of tests you would perform. For each test, explain what result would narrow the location of the lesion and what result would broaden it. How would a positive Babinski sign on the left change your assessment compared to a negative Babinski sign? How would aphasia (language loss) change your assessment? How would loss of temperature sensation on the left arm change your assessment?

## Chapter Summary

The neurological exam is a clinical tool for rapid localization of damage or disease in the nervous system. It is organized around five major sections, each probing a specific region of the central nervous system: the cerebral cortex (mental status), the brainstem (cranial nerves), the spinal cord (sensation and motor function), and the cerebellum (coordination). The exam converts behavior into anatomy. By asking the patient to perform specific tasks—follow a pen with your eyes, feel this touch, remember three words, push against resistance—the examiner can determine which nervous system structures are not functioning properly. The power of this approach lies in the principle of localization of function: specific regions of the nervous system are responsible for specific functions, and loss of a function points to damage in the region responsible for it. The cranial nerve exam is a tour through the brainstem, and the pattern of deficit—which nerves are affected and which are spared—narrows the location of the lesion. The motor exam distinguishes upper motor neuron disease from lower motor neuron disease through tests of muscle tone, strength, reflexes, and the Babinski sign, each of which reveals whether the lesion is in the cortex or brainstem (upper motor neuron) or in the spinal cord or peripheral nerve (lower motor neuron). The sensory exam, organized by dermatome and sensory modality, can localize lesions to specific spinal cord levels or to ascending pathways in the brainstem and thalamus. The coordination exam tests cerebellar function and can reveal ataxia—loss of coordinated movement. Together, these five sections form an integrated system for converting patient symptoms into anatomical diagnosis. In the emergency department, the neurological exam provides critical information within minutes. In the classroom, it demonstrates how structure and function in the nervous system are inseparable.

## Connections Forward

Damage to the nervous system comes from many sources: stroke (loss of blood flow to brain tissue), hemorrhage (bleeding into the brain), trauma (head injury), infection (meningitis, encephalitis), toxins (alcohol, mercury, carbon monoxide), genetic disease (Huntington's disease, multiple sclerosis), and neurodegenerative disease (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's). Each produces a different pattern of neurological deficits. A patient with Alzheimer's disease will show progressive memory loss and decline in mental status, but preserved motor function and reflexes, until late in the disease. A patient with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) will show loss of motor function with upper motor neuron signs (spasticity, hyperreflexia) in the upper extremities and lower motor neuron signs (flaccidity, absent reflexes) in the lower extremities. A patient with multiple sclerosis might show virtually any combination of deficits, depending on the location of demyelinated plaques. Understanding the neurological exam—understanding localization of function—is the first step toward understanding disease. It is the foundation on which all clinical neurology is built.

## What Would Change My Mind

This chapter frames the neurological exam as a localization tool based on structure-function relationships. Evidence that would revise this reading: a demonstration that lesions in a given anatomical location commonly produce deficits in structures thought to be functionally independent would challenge the localization principle. Discovery of extensive redundancy—multiple pathways serving single functions such that lesions produce minimal deficits—would suggest the exam is less precise than presented. A clinical study showing that neurological deficits do not correlate with imaging findings more often than current literature suggests would indicate the exam is less reliable than clinical practice assumes.

## Still Puzzling

The cerebellum's role extends beyond motor coordination to procedural learning and possibly affective processing, yet these functions remain incompletely understood. The nature of consciousness itself—why some cortical lesions produce obvious deficits while others do not—remains mysterious. How the brain compensates for lesions through reorganization and plasticity is an active area of research and not fully captured by the static model of localization the exam assumes.
