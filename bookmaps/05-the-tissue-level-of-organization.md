**What was added:**

## PEDAGOGICAL MOVES: SOURCE vs. CHAPTER

### Move 1: The Hook

**Source:** Starts with definitions. "The term tissue is used to describe a group of cells found together in the body."

**Chapter:** Starts in scene. "You are standing at the boundary of your own body. Not the skin — go deeper. Look at the intestinal lining..."

**Reason:** Attenborough × Feynman style demands present-tense immersion before abstraction. The source is accurate but inert.

### Move 2: Specification

**Source:** Defines tissue types but doesn't force specificity. "Epithelial tissue... refers to the sheets of cells..."

**Chapter:** Forces the question "what does this actually do?" Epithelium is described as a "decision-maker" from the first mention.

**Reason:** Specification is a teaching move. Asking "what does this specifically mean?" often resolves confusion before it starts.

### Move 3: Mechanism

**Source:** Lists junctions. "Tight junctions... Anchoring junctions... Gap junctions..."

**Chapter:** Explains the mechanism of each. "Two proteins called claudins and occludins interweave... like a zipper."

**Reason:** Understanding how something works is deeper than knowing what it's called. The source lists; the chapter explains mechanism.

### Move 4: Trade-Off

**Source:** Presents properties without framing the cost. "Epithelial tissues are thin" (true but incomplete).

**Chapter:** Names the trade-off explicitly. "The trade-off: epithelial tissues are durable but thin. They must be thin... But thinness means fragility."

**Reason:** Understanding trade-offs is understanding engineering logic. Evolution (and design) always makes choices. Naming both sides of the choice is respect for the complexity.

### Move 5: Scale Shift

**Source:** No explicit scale shifts.

**Chapter:** Moves from molecular (a glucose molecule) to cellular (a single epithelial cell deciding) to tissue (a sheet of millions) to organ (the intestine).

**Reason:** Showing where something sits in the nested structure of the body helps students understand both the intimacy and the scope.

## WHAT THE SOURCE DOES WELL

1. **Comprehensiveness.** The source covers all four tissue types, all major classifications, and important examples.
2. **Clarity of structure.** The source's organization (Define → Classify → Give Examples → Review Questions) is pedagogically sound.
3. **Terminology.** The source is precise with terminology and defines terms on first use (mostly).
4. **Examples.** The source uses concrete anatomical locations (lung alveoli, kidney tubules, etc.) to ground abstraction.
5. **Images.** The OpenStax images are high-quality histological photographs.

---

## WHAT THE SOURCE LACKS (Addressed in Chapter)

1. **Narrative hook.** The source begins with definitions. The chapter begins in scene.
2. **Mechanism.** The source lists structures; the chapter explains how they work.
3. **Trade-offs.** The source describes properties; the chapter names what's sacrificed for what's gained.
4. **Scale shifts.** The source stays at tissue scale; the chapter moves between molecular, cellular, tissue, and organ scales.
5. **Integration.** The source treats tissues as separate categories; the chapter shows how they require each other.
6. **Speculation.** The source is careful about limitations; the chapter names what's still puzzling.

## CRITICAL OMISSIONS (Intentional)

### Embryonic Origin (Germ Layer Mapping)
**Source (m46046):**
- Ectoderm → epithelium (skin, mouth, anus), nervous tissue
- Mesoderm → connective tissue, muscle tissue, epithelium (vessels)
- Endoderm → epithelium (digestive, respiratory)

**Chapter treatment:**
- Moved to "Prerequisites" section (assumes knowledge from Ch 4)
- Not developed in the chapter body (could clutter the narrative)

**Reason:** The germ layer origin is important for developmental understanding but not necessary for understanding tissue function. Students who have completed Ch 4 already know this. Repeating it would slow the chapter.

### Tissue Membranes (Serous, Mucous, Cutaneous)
**Source (m46046):** Details the types and locations of tissue membranes.

**Chapter treatment:**
- Mentioned in opening prerequisites as part of epithelium's role
- Not developed (no dedicated section)

**Reason:** Tissue membranes are important anatomically but are compositions of the basic tissues already covered (epithelium + connective tissue). The detail adds classification burden without adding mechanistic understanding.

### Wound Healing and Tissue Repair
**Source (m46058):** Details the phases of wound healing, scar formation, and regeneration.

**Chapter treatment:**
- Not included (deferred to later chapters and clinical contexts)

**Reason:** Tissue repair is a whole chapter's worth of detail. It appears in the chapter as a case study (ACL healing) but not as a general mechanism. Future chapters on specific tissues (bone, muscle, skin) will detail repair for those tissues.

### Tissue Aging
**Source (m46058):** Discusses how tissues change with age (collagen cross-linking, reduced elasticity, etc.).

**Chapter treatment:**
- Not included

**Reason:** Aging is a system-level phenomenon that appears better in chapters on specific tissues and organs. For a foundational chapter, it adds complexity without teaching mechanism.
