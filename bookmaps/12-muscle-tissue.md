## Key Ideas Harvested for Conversion

### 1. The Unified Mechanism (Move 1: Puzzle)

**Source provides:** Three muscle types with different structures.
**Conversion amplified:** All three use identical sliding filament mechanism; differences are in control, not engine.
**Teaching value:** Single mechanism underlying all movement unifies the chapter. Trade-offs explain structural differences.

### 2. Calcium as a Gate, Not a Power Source (Move 2: Specification)

**Source says:** "Calcium enters the sarcoplasm and binds to troponin, forcing tropomyosin to move aside so that the actin-binding sites are available."
**Conversion clarifies:** Calcium is binary (gate open/closed), not a power amplifier. Force comes from number of cross-bridges, not strength per bridge.
**Why it matters:** Student misconception that "more calcium = stronger contraction" is prevented by clear specification.

### 3. ATP's Multiple Jobs (Move 3: First Principles)

**Source lists separately:** ATP recocks heads, pumps calcium, detaches cross-bridges.
**Conversion reorganizes:** All are rate-limiting factors. Without ATP, contraction cannot start (no recock), cannot continue (no new cross-bridges), cannot relax (no detachment). Rigor mortis example shows this.

### 4. The T-Tubule Depth Problem (Move 3: First Principles)

**Source says:** "T-tubules ensure that the membrane can get close to the SR."
**Conversion explains:** Large muscle fiber diameter creates a depth problem. T-tubules solve it by bringing action potential deep into cell synchronously. Without them, interior would lag. Quantified: 100 micrometers diameter, ~20 microseconds to full activation.

### 5. Length-Tension Relationship as Design Constraint (Move 2: Specification)

**Source shows:** Force drops if stretched or compressed beyond optimal range.
**Conversion specifies:** Optimal at 80–120% resting length. Stretched = filaments separate = no overlap = no cross-bridges. Compressed = thin filaments interfere = fewer binding sites. This is a design limit, not a failure.

### 6. Three Muscle Types as Control Solutions (Move 1 & 3: Puzzle + Mechanism)

**Source presents:** Three types with different structures.
**Conversion frames:** Same engine, three control strategies solving three different problems.
- Skeletal: voluntary, fast (milliseconds), moderate fatigue
- Cardiac: involuntary, rhythmic (via pacemakers), fatigue-resistant
- Smooth: involuntary, sustained (via latch-bridges), energy-efficient

### 7. Motor Units and Recruitment (Move 2: Specification)

**Source explains:** Motor neuron and its muscle fibers; size affects control precision.
**Conversion quantifies:** Small motor unit = 6 fibers per neuron (eyes) = fine control. Large motor unit = 1000 fibers per neuron (legs) = gross movement. Recruitment principle: small units fire first, larger units added for more force.

### 8. Smooth Muscle Calmodulin Cascade (Move 3: First Principles)

**Source describes:** Calcium + calmodulin activates myosin kinase, which phosphorylates myosin heads.
**Conversion explains:** This is more complex than skeletal muscle's troponin gate, but achieves the same goal: regulate binding site availability. Key difference: phosphorylation is slower than troponin conformational change.

### 9. Latch-Bridges as Energy Savings (Move 3: First Principles)

**Source mentions:** "Some cross-bridges cycle slowly without ATP."
**Conversion explains:** Dephosphorylation causes myosin heads to remain partially bound (latch state). Filaments stay linked with minimal ATP cycling. Advantage: sustained tension for hours (arterial tone). Disadvantage: slowness (can't be used in voluntary muscle).

### 10. Rigor Mortis as Teaching Tool (Move 1: Puzzle)

**Source notes:** "With no further ATP production, there is no ATP available for myosin heads to detach from the actin-binding sites, so the cross-bridges stay in place, causing the rigidity."
**Conversion uses:** As evidence that ATP is required continuously, not just to start contraction. Also explains what ATP actually does (detachment and recock), not what it's commonly believed to do (power the power stroke).

## Misconceptions from Source (And How to Correct Them)

1. **Calcium "activates" contraction** → Calcium gates binding sites; doesn't amplify force
2. **ATP powers the power stroke** → ATP powers recock and detachment; phosphate release powers stroke
3. **Smooth muscle is disorganized** → It's organized differently (dense bodies, looser lattice)
4. **Muscle fatigue is single-cause** → Source lists many contributing factors; specification is key
5. **Motor units are all the same** → Source notes size variation but could emphasize recruitment hierarchy

## Scale Oscillations Present in Source (Attenborough Move)

- Organ level: heart, stomach, arm (opening imagery)
- Tissue level: muscle, fascia, tendon
- Fiber level: multinucleated cylinders (skeletal), branched networks (cardiac), spindle-shaped (smooth)
- Myofibril level: 1.2 micrometer diameter, hundreds per fiber
- Sarcomere level: 2 micrometer length, Z-disc to Z-disc
- Molecular level: myosin head, actin molecule, troponin conformational change

## Cold Opens in Source (Attenborough Move 1)

- "Tennis Player" image (action-based)
- Skeletal muscle "visible just under the skin" (concrete)
- Cardiac muscle "concerned with pumping blood" (function-based)
- Smooth muscle "having one's hair stand on end" and "moving food through digestive system" (lived experience)

**Conversion amplifies:** Opens Concept 1 with sprinter's footfall vs. heart's steady beat vs. stomach's slow squeeze—all same machinery, different control. This is the puzzle that unifies the chapter.

## Trade-Offs Present in Source

- **Skeletal:** Fast control (voluntary, milliseconds) vs. ATP cost (calcium pumps run constantly)
- **Cardiac:** Endurance (pacemaker-driven, fatigue-resistant) vs. Loss of voluntary control
- **Smooth:** Low energy cost (latch-bridges, sustained tension) vs. Slowness (seconds to reach peak)
- **ATP sources:** Creatine phosphate (fast, limited), glycolysis (moderate, limited), aerobic (slow, abundant)
- **Fiber types:** Slow oxidative (endurance, low force) vs. fast glycolytic (explosive, tires quickly)

## Analogies Present (Implicit) in Source

- Rowing (myosin heads pulling): source notes "similar to oars when a rower pulls, is lifted from the water, repositioned, and immersed again"
- Zipper (thin/thick filaments sliding): implicit in "sliding filament" description
- Relay race (motor unit recruitment): implicit in "large motor units can generate 50 times more force"
