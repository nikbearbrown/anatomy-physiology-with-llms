# Images — Chapter 3: The Chemical Level of Organization

## Concept 1: Atoms, Stability, and Bonding

### [FIGURE: Electron Shell Diagrams—Helium, Carbon, Oxygen]
**Placement:** After the Mechanism section, before the trade-off.
**Description:** Three simple Bohr models showing electron shells as concentric circles. Helium: nucleus with 2 protons, 2 electrons in first shell (full). Carbon: nucleus with 6 protons, 2 electrons in first shell (full), 4 in second shell (half-full). Oxygen: nucleus with 8 protons, 2 in first shell, 6 in second shell (mostly full).
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Visual reinforcement of the valence shell concept. The reader sees immediately why carbon is reactive (needs 4 more) and helium is inert (full shell).
**Style Notes:** Clean, simple line drawings. No shading or 3D effects. Labels show atomic number (protons) and valence electrons. Color code: nucleus (dark), inner shell electrons (one color), valence electrons (another color).

### [FIGURE: Covalent Bond Formation—H₂O]
**Placement:** In the worked example section, showing oxygen and hydrogen bonding.
**Description:** Left side: separate oxygen and hydrogen atoms with valence electrons shown. Middle: arrows showing electron sharing. Right side: water molecule with shared electron pairs between O and H bonds depicted as overlap.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows the mechanism of covalent bonding without invoking quantum mechanics. The shared pair is the bond.
**Style Notes:** Animated progression (left → middle → right) works well in digital. Show electron clouds around the nuclei to hint at reality while keeping mechanics clear.

### [FIGURE: Isotopes of Carbon]
**Placement:** In the Mechanism section, after discussing atomic number and mass number.
**Description:** Three carbon atoms side by side. All have 6 protons and 6 electrons. Carbon-12 has 6 neutrons. Carbon-13 has 7 neutrons. Carbon-14 has 8 neutrons. Neutrons are shown as distinct from protons in the nucleus.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Illustrates that isotopes are the same element (same protons) but different mass numbers (different neutrons). Sets up radioactivity as a natural phenomenon.
**Style Notes:** Protons and neutrons should be visually distinct in the nucleus. Use shading or color to differentiate.

## Concept 2: Water—The Solvent and Matrix of Life

### [FIGURE: Polar Water Molecule and Hydrogen Bonding]
**Placement:** In the Mechanism section, after discussing polarity.
**Description:** Single water molecule showing bent geometry, with δ+ on hydrogen ends and δ- on oxygen. Second image shows three water molecules with dashed hydrogen bonds forming a network. The bonds should be labeled as hydrogen bonds (weaker than covalent).
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Visually shows that (a) water is bent, not linear, (b) polarity creates partial charges, (c) partial charges enable hydrogen bonding.
**Style Notes:** Use δ+ and δ- notation. Hydrogen bonds depicted as dashed lines, covalent bonds as solid lines. Color helps: use same color for all hydrogen bonds to show the pattern.

### [FIGURE: Ion Hydration—Salt Dissolving]
**Placement:** In the worked example section.
**Description:** Salt crystal (lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions) on the left. Water molecules clustering around the surface. Na⁺ ions surrounded by water with negative ends pointing in. Cl⁻ ions surrounded by water with positive ends pointing in. Right side shows dissolved ions separated by water molecules.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows why water is a "universal solvent" for ionic compounds. The polarity of water overcome the ionic bonds in the crystal.
**Style Notes:** Animate if possible: crystal → water approaching → ions separating → dispersed in solution. This sequence is memorable.

### [FIGURE: Hydrophobic vs. Hydrophilic Interactions]
**Placement:** In the Mechanism section, after discussing water as solvent.
**Description:** Two scenarios. Top: hydrophilic molecule (polar, with charges) surrounded by water molecules. Water molecules orient their partial charges toward the molecule's charges. Bottom: hydrophobic molecule (nonpolar) surrounded by water. Water molecules form a shell around the hydrophobic molecule, orienting toward each other instead of the molecule. Show that water "prefers" to bond with water, so hydrophobic substances cluster together to minimize water contact.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Explains why oils do not dissolve in water and why phospholipids form bilayers.
**Style Notes:** Use color to distinguish hydrophilic (charged or polar groups) from hydrophobic (nonpolar backbone). Dashed lines show water bonding to water; straight lines show water-solute interactions.

## Concept 3: Macromolecules—Structure and Function

### [FIGURE: Dehydration Synthesis and Hydrolysis]
**Placement:** In the Mechanism section, early in the macromolecules concept.
**Description:** Top half: two monomers approaching. One has a hydroxyl group (-OH), the other a hydrogen (H). They bond, releasing H₂O. The result is a dimer. Bottom half: the reverse. Water molecule breaks the bond, donating H to one monomer and OH to the other. This is hydrolysis.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows that assembly and disassembly are opposite processes, both involving water. This is the mechanism underlying polymer growth and breakdown.
**Style Notes:** Use color to track atoms. Show the water molecule being released in synthesis, being consumed in hydrolysis. Arrows should be bidirectional to show reversibility.

### [FIGURE: Four Types of Carbohydrates]
**Placement:** In the Carbohydrates subsection.
**Description:** Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose as hexagon structures; ribose, deoxyribose as pentagon structures). Disaccharides (sucrose as two glucose-like rings joined; lactose; maltose). Polysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose as long chains). Show the increasing complexity.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Visual progression from simple (monosaccharides) to increasingly complex (polysaccharides). Helps reader distinguish between dietary carbs (disaccharides like sucrose) and stored carbs (polysaccharides like glycogen).
**Style Notes:** Use different colors for different monosaccharide types. Show glycosidic bonds clearly (the bond formed between monosaccharides). For polysaccharides, show that the backbone is the same; the difference is in branching (amylose vs. amylopectin) or in chain conformation (starch vs. cellulose).

### [FIGURE: Fatty Acid Structures—Saturated vs. Unsaturated]
**Placement:** In the Lipids subsection, in the Triglycerides section.
**Description:** Two fatty acids side by side. Saturated: straight chain of carbons, each bonded to two hydrogens. Unsaturated: chain with a double bond (C=C), causing a kink in the chain. Show how straight chains pack together (solid at room temperature) vs. kinked chains that cannot pack tightly (liquid at room temperature).
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows why saturated fats are solid and unsaturated fats are liquid. Explains the chemical basis of the fat-oil distinction.
**Style Notes:** Saturated: straight line. Unsaturated: zigzag pattern showing the kink at the double bond. Use color to highlight the double bond. Show multiple molecules packed together in saturated case, spread apart in unsaturated case.

### [FIGURE: Triglyceride Structure]
**Placement:** In the Lipids subsection, Triglycerides section.
**Description:** Glycerol backbone (three-carbon chain) with three fatty acid chains attached via ester bonds. Show the dehydration synthesis that builds the triglyceride (three water molecules released).
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows the composition of a fat molecule: one glycerol, three fatty acids. Explains how triglycerides form from their components.
**Style Notes:** Glycerol in one color, each fatty acid in another. Ester bonds (C-O bonds) should be labeled. Show that the triglyceride is a lipid because it is mostly hydrocarbon with few polar groups.

### [FIGURE: Phospholipid Bilayer]
**Placement:** In the Lipids subsection, Phospholipids section.
**Description:** Individual phospholipid showing polar head (phosphate group) and nonpolar tails (fatty acids). Then assembly of phospholipids into a bilayer: heads face outward (toward water), tails face inward (away from water). This is the foundation of cell membranes.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows why phospholipids form bilayers: they are amphipathic (both polar and nonpolar). The structure is a direct consequence of their chemistry.
**Style Notes:** Heads as circles or spheres (to emphasize their spherical polar region), tails as lines or squiggles (to show their nonpolar character). Bilayer should show the water environment outside and inside, emphasizing that heads face water.

### [FIGURE: Amino Acid Structure and Peptide Bond]
**Placement:** In the Proteins subsection, Microstructure section.
**Description:** Single amino acid showing the amino group (-NH₂), carboxyl group (-COOH), and variable side chain (R-group). Show several amino acids with different side chains (glycine with H, leucine with a branched hydrocarbon, cysteine with sulfur). Then show two amino acids bonding via dehydration synthesis to form a peptide bond.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows that proteins are built from amino acids, and that the side chain determines each amino acid's properties. The variety of side chains explains protein diversity.
**Style Notes:** Color-code the three constant parts (amino, carboxyl, central carbon) in one color. Side chains in another. Show the peptide bond as a C-N covalent bond formed by dehydration synthesis.

### [FIGURE: Protein Structure Hierarchy]
**Placement:** In the Proteins subsection, Shape section.
**Description:** Primary structure: linear sequence of amino acids. Secondary structure: alpha-helix (spiral held by hydrogen bonds within the chain) or beta-pleated sheet (zigzag pattern held by hydrogen bonds between chains). Tertiary structure: compact 3D shape. Quaternary structure: multiple subunits (e.g., hemoglobin with four subunits).
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows how proteins fold from linear chains to compact machines. Each level of structure emerges from interactions between amino acids.
**Style Notes:** Use a single protein as example throughout (e.g., hemoglobin). Show that the sequence (primary) determines the fold (secondary), which determines the shape (tertiary), which enables quaternary assembly. Color-code different structural elements.

### [FIGURE: Enzyme-Substrate Complex]
**Placement:** In the Proteins subsection, Enzymes section.
**Description:** Enzyme with active site. Substrate approaching. Substrate fitting into active site (induced fit: enzyme shape changes slightly). Reaction occurring. Product released. Enzyme returns to original shape.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows that enzymes are shape-specific catalysts. They lower activation energy by positioning substrates optimally for reaction.
**Style Notes:** Enzyme as large protein, substrate as small molecule. Active site should be drawn as a pocket or crevice. Induced fit is key: show the enzyme shape changing as the substrate binds. After product release, enzyme should return to original shape, ready for another round.

### [FIGURE: DNA Double Helix]
**Placement:** In the Nucleotides section, Nucleic Acids.
**Description:** DNA as a double helix. Show the sugar-phosphate backbone (the rails of the helix). Show the base pairs inside (adenine-thymine, guanine-cytosine, held by hydrogen bonds). Show the helical twist.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Visually represents the structure that stores genetic information. The base-pairing pattern explains how DNA replicates.
**Style Notes:** Use different colors for different base pairs (A-T vs. G-C). Hydrogen bonds as dashed lines (weaker than the covalent bonds in the backbone). The helix should show the major and minor grooves.

### [FIGURE: ATP Structure and Energy Release]
**Placement:** In the Nucleotides section, ATP subsection.
**Description:** ATP molecule showing the ribose sugar, the adenine base, and three phosphate groups. Highlight the high-energy phosphate bonds (the bonds between phosphate groups). Show ATP → ADP + Pi + energy. Show the reverse: ADP + Pi + energy → ATP (phosphorylation).
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows that ATP is a nucleotide with high-energy bonds. Breaking those bonds releases energy for cellular work.
**Style Notes:** Phosphate groups in one color, adenine in another, ribose in another. High-energy bonds should be emphasized (thicker lines, different color). The energy released should be labeled with a numerical value (e.g., 7.3 kcal/mole) or shown as a lightning bolt or similar metaphor.

## Sickle Cell Hemoglobin (Cross-Concept Image)

### [FIGURE: Normal vs. Sickle Cell Hemoglobin]
**Placement:** In the Proteins subsection, as part of the worked example on protein shape.
**Description:** Normal hemoglobin: spherical, smooth surface. Sickle cell hemoglobin: glutamic acid at position 6 (charged, hydrophilic) is replaced by valine (nonpolar, hydrophobic). This creates an exposed nonpolar patch on the surface. Under low oxygen, multiple hemoglobin molecules polymerize through these nonpolar patches, forming long fibers. The red blood cell, wrapped around these fibers, sickling.
**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows how one amino acid change can destroy protein function. The mechanism (exposure of nonpolar patch, polymerization under low oxygen) is visible in the structure.
**Style Notes:** Normal hemoglobin: smooth, colored uniformly. Sickle hemoglobin: same shape but with a highlighted patch where valine replaces glutamic acid. Show multiple hemoglobin molecules linking through this patch. Show the red blood cell distorted by the fibers inside.

## Image Integration Notes

- **All figures should be clear enough to read and understand in black-and-white.** Assume some students print them.
- **Progression within each concept:** Simple (atoms) → intermediate (bonds) → complex (macromolecules). Images should reflect this progression.
- **Consistency:** Use the same notation for atoms, bonds, and functional groups throughout. Hydrogen bonds are always dashed lines. Covalent bonds are always solid lines.
- **Labeling:** Every image should be fully labeled. A student should be able to understand the image without reading the text (though the text deepens understanding).
- **Interactive potential:** Many of these images could be animated in a digital version (hydration shell forming, dehydration synthesis occurring, enzyme-substrate complex cycling). Static versions should be sufficient for print.
