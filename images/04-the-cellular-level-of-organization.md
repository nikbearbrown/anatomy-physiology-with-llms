# Chapter 04 Images & Figures Guide

## Image Specifications & Placement

### 1. Red Blood Cell Osmosis Sequence (Chapter Opening)

**Placement:** After chapter opening scene description (page break)

**Format:** Three-panel horizontal sequence, or single animation-style image showing progression

**Visual Description:**
- Panel A: RBC in pure water, beginning to swell (cell edges bulging outward)
- Panel B: RBC heavily swollen, membrane about to rupture (cell distended)
- Panel C: Lysed RBC — ghost cell, contents spilled, pale hollow membrane remaining

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Anchors osmosis in observable phenomenon. Shows consequence of osmotic imbalance. Motivates the need for selective permeability and osmotic regulation.

**Style Notes:** Use realistic cell appearance (disc-like in resting state, not a cartoon). Show water molecules or indicate water flow with arrows for clarity. Include labels: "Pure water (hypotonic)," "Net water flow," "Hemoglobin released."

**Caption:** "A red blood cell placed in pure water begins to swell as water enters by osmosis. The membrane cannot withstand the pressure and ruptures, releasing hemoglobin. This is why isotonic intravenous solutions are critical in medical practice."

### 2. Phospholipid Structure (Concept 1 - Mechanism)

**Placement:** Immediately before or after first discussion of phospholipid head and tail structure

**Format:** Side-by-side comparison or molecular diagram

**Visual Description:**
- Left: Single phospholipid molecule shown in 2D or 3D
  - Polar head group (phosphate) clearly labeled and shown with charges (δ+ on nitrogen, δ- on phosphate)
  - Two nonpolar fatty acid tails extending downward, shown as wavy lines or ball-and-stick
  - Arrow pointing to each component with labels
- Right (optional): Comparison showing head is hydrophilic (surrounded by water molecules), tail is hydrophobic (repels water)

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Establishes the amphipathic nature of phospholipid. Visual understanding of why bilayer forms. Foundation for all subsequent membrane discussion.

**Style Notes:** Color-code: head group in bright color (blue/red for charges), tails in neutral tan/gray. Show water molecules around hydrophilic head to make hydrophilicity concrete.

**Caption:** "A phospholipid molecule has a charged, water-loving head and two water-repelling fatty acid tails. When placed in aqueous solution, phospholipids arrange themselves with heads facing water and tails hiding from water. This molecular geometry determines how membranes form."

### 3. Phospholipid Bilayer (Concept 1 - Mechanism)

**Placement:** After phospholipid structure, before transport discussion

**Format:** Cross-section view of bilayer, showing both inside and outside

**Visual Description:**
- Horizontal layers: two rows of phospholipids
- Inner row: tails pointing upward (toward inside)
- Outer row: tails pointing downward (toward outside)
- Heads meeting water: hydrophilic heads on top surface face extracellular fluid; heads on bottom surface face intracellular fluid (cytoplasm)
- Hydrophobic interior clearly shown as a barrier
- Proteins embedded within (optional on this figure, can be on separate figure)
- Water molecules shown outside on both sides, not penetrating interior

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Visual foundation for understanding selective permeability. Shows why nonpolar molecules cross, polar molecules cannot. Sets up next concept (transport).

**Style Notes:** Use gradient coloring to show hydrophobic interior (lighter, less polar) vs. hydrophilic exterior (darker, more charged). Arrows show directionality of molecule orientation.

**Caption:** "The cell membrane consists of two layers of phospholipids arranged back-to-back. Hydrophilic heads face the aqueous environments both inside and outside the cell. Hydrophobic tails meet in the middle, forming a barrier to water-soluble substances. Cholesterol molecules (not shown) are interspersed within the tails, regulating fluidity."

### 4. Membrane Proteins and Selective Permeability (Concept 1 - Mechanism)

**Placement:** After bilayer figure, when discussing integral and peripheral proteins

**Format:** Cross-section of bilayer with proteins embedded

**Visual Description:**
- Phospholipid bilayer as background (simplified or in gray)
- Three types of proteins labeled:
  - **Integral protein (channel protein):** Spans entire membrane, forming a pore; show small molecules or ions passing through
  - **Receptor protein:** Embedded in bilayer, extending slightly outside, with ligand (hormone or neurotransmitter) docking to it
  - **Peripheral protein:** On surface of bilayer, not spanning; may be attached to integral protein or lipid
- Show carbohydrate chains on glycoproteins (forming glycocalyx)
- Indicate hydrophobic vs. hydrophilic regions of proteins

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows that the membrane is not just lipid; proteins are essential for specificity and function. Introduces concept of protein-mediated transport, ion channels, receptors.

**Style Notes:** Use different colors for different protein types. Show charged regions of proteins interacting favorably with aqueous environment; hydrophobic regions interact with lipid tails. Include labels for each protein type and its function.

**Caption:** "Cell membranes contain proteins that span the bilayer or attach to its surface. Integral channel proteins allow specific ions to cross. Receptor proteins bind hormones and neurotransmitters, triggering intracellular signals. Peripheral proteins serve as structural supports or enzymes. Together, lipids and proteins form a dynamic, selective barrier."

### 5. Osmosis in Three Solution Types (Concept 1 - Worked Example)

**Placement:** After osmosis description

**Format:** Three-panel comparison, showing cell in hypotonic, isotonic, and hypertonic solutions

**Visual Description:**
- Each panel shows a red blood cell and surrounding solution
- **Hypotonic (left panel):** 
  - Cell swollen, membrane bulging
  - Blue arrows showing water flowing inward
  - Solution labeled "Low solute concentration"
- **Isotonic (center panel):**
  - Cell in normal disc shape
  - Small arrows showing equal water movement in and out (or no net movement indicated)
  - Solution labeled "Equal solute concentration (0.9% NaCl)"
- **Hypertonic (right panel):**
  - Cell shriveled, wrinkled edges (crenation)
  - Blue arrows showing water flowing outward
  - Solution labeled "High solute concentration"

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Consolidates understanding of osmosis. Shows three clinically relevant scenarios. Motivates understanding of homeostatic regulation of body fluid osmolarity.

**Style Notes:** Cell shape changes should be visually obvious and exaggerated slightly for clarity. Use arrows consistently to show water movement. Include numerical solute concentrations.

**Caption:** "Water moves across the cell membrane by osmosis, following the concentration of dissolved solutes. In hypotonic solutions (fewer solutes outside), water flows in and cells swell. In isotonic solutions (equal solutes), water moves equally in both directions. In hypertonic solutions (more solutes outside), water flows out and cells shrink. The body maintains isotonic conditions in extracellular fluid to prevent cell damage."

### 6. Passive Transport: Simple and Facilitated Diffusion (Concept 2 - Mechanism)

**Placement:** When introducing passive transport

**Format:** Two-panel comparison

**Visual Description:**
- **Simple Diffusion (left):**
  - Oxygen and CO₂ molecules shown passing directly through phospholipid bilayer
  - No protein needed
  - Concentration gradient shown (more molecules on left, fewer on right)
  - Molecules moving from high to low concentration
- **Facilitated Diffusion (right):**
  - Glucose molecules shown inside membrane proteins (channels or carriers)
  - Proteins shown specifically selecting glucose, excluding other sugars
  - Concentration gradient shown (glucose more concentrated outside)
  - Glucose moving from high to low concentration through protein

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Distinguishes between transport requiring no protein (simple) and transport requiring selectivity (facilitated). Both are passive (no ATP). Sets up active transport contrast.

**Style Notes:** Show only the relevant molecules and proteins. Use arrows to show concentration gradient and molecular movement. Color-code: simple diffusion molecules in one color (blue), facilitated diffusion molecules in another (red), proteins in neutral color.

**Caption:** "Passive transport moves substances down their concentration gradient without using cellular energy. Simple diffusion allows small, nonpolar molecules like oxygen to cross the lipid bilayer directly. Facilitated diffusion uses membrane proteins to transport larger or charged molecules like glucose, which cannot pass through the bilayer alone. Both processes continue until equilibrium is reached."

### 7. Active Transport: Sodium-Potassium Pump (Concept 2 - Mechanism)

**Placement:** When describing active transport and the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase

**Format:** Cyclical diagram showing the six steps of the pump cycle

**Visual Description:**
- Central protein shown in two conformations (E1 and E2 states)
- Six arrows forming a cycle, labeled Step 1–6:
  1. ATP binds to pump (inside cell)
  2. Na⁺ binds (3 ions shown), ATP hydrolyzes
  3. Phosphate attaches, pump flips (extracellular-facing)
  4. Na⁺ released outside, K⁺ binds (2 ions shown)
  5. Pump flips back (intracellular-facing), phosphate released
  6. K⁺ released inside, ready for next cycle
- Concentration gradients shown: Na⁺ high outside/low inside; K⁺ low outside/high inside
- Energy indicator: ATP → ADP + Pi, showing energy input

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows the mechanical work of active transport. Explains the 3-for-2 exchange and why inside is negative. Demonstrates ATP coupling. Critical for understanding resting potential.

**Style Notes:** Use two distinct colors for Na⁺ (purple) and K⁺ (orange) to track them through the cycle. Show ATP breakdown with visual clarity. Indicate conformational changes of pump.

**Caption:** "The sodium-potassium pump actively transports ions against their concentration gradients, using one ATP molecule per cycle. It removes three sodium ions from the cytoplasm and brings two potassium ions in. This net outward pumping of positive charge makes the inside of the cell negative, creating the resting potential. The pump consumes enormous amounts of ATP — in neurons, up to 40% of cellular ATP is spent on this single process."

### 8. Cystic Fibrosis Cascade (Concept 2 - Worked Example)

**Placement:** After CF description

**Format:** Flow chart or decision tree showing normal vs. CF pathway

**Visual Description:**
- **Top row (Normal):**
  - Gene → mRNA → CFTR protein
  - Protein inserts in membrane
  - Cl⁻ pumped out
  - Na⁺ follows
  - H₂O follows (osmosis)
  - Thin, slippery mucus
  - Cilia move mucus upward
  - Bacteria cleared
- **Bottom row (CF):**
  - Gene → mRNA → Defective CFTR
  - Protein misfolds
  - Quality control degrades protein
  - No Cl⁻ transport
  - No Na⁺ or H₂O movement
  - Thick, sticky mucus
  - Cilia cannot move mucus
  - Bacteria colonize
  - Chronic infections → scarring → lung damage

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Traces molecular defect to physiological consequence. Shows where therapeutic interventions could occur. Emphasizes importance of understanding transport.

**Style Notes:** Use color to distinguish normal (green) from defective (red) pathway. Include icons or simplified drawings for each step. Show cascading consequences visually.

**Caption:** "Cystic fibrosis results from mutations in the CFTR gene, which encodes a chloride transporter. When the protein misfolds and is degraded, chloride cannot be pumped into the mucus layer. Without chloride, sodium and water do not follow, leaving the mucus dehydrated, thick, and sticky. Cilia cannot move this mucus, leading to airway blockage, bacterial colonization, and progressive lung damage. Potential therapies target each step: gene therapy (fix the gene), chaperone therapy (help protein fold correctly), or bypass strategies (activate alternative chloride channels)."

### 9. Organelles of the Endomembrane System (Concept 3 - Mechanism)

**Placement:** When introducing rough ER, Golgi, lysosomes, and vesicular transport

**Format:** Simplified cutaway view of cell showing ER-Golgi-lysosome pathway

**Visual Description:**
- Nuclear envelope at top/center
- Rough ER (with ribosomes shown as dots) continuous with nuclear envelope
- Ribosomes actively translating proteins (show mRNA in ribosome)
- Vesicles budding from ER with newly synthesized proteins
- Vesicles transporting to Golgi apparatus
- Golgi shown as stack of flattened sacs (cisternae)
- Vesicles receiving at cis-face, exiting from trans-face
- Trans-Golgi network sending vesicles to:
  - Plasma membrane (secretory vesicles)
  - Back to ER (retrieval vesicles)
  - Lysosome (enzyme-containing vesicles)
- Lysosomes shown as dense vesicles with enzymatic content
- Fusion of endocytic vesicles with lysosomes

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows the integrated nature of the endomembrane system. Demonstrates vesicular transport as the mechanism of intracellular communication. Essential for understanding protein secretion and degradation.

**Style Notes:** Use arrows to show vesicle movement and cargo transport. Color-code different vesicle types if needed. Show ribosomes as small dots on rough ER. Include organelle names and key proteins.

**Caption:** "The endomembrane system is a network of membrane-bound compartments that manufactures, modifies, and distributes cellular products. Proteins synthesized on rough ER are transported in vesicles to the Golgi apparatus, where they are sorted and modified. Vesicles then carry them to the plasma membrane for secretion or to lysosomes for degradation. This system operates continuously, with thousands of vesicles budding, transporting, and fusing every second."

### 10. Mitochondrial Structure and ATP Synthesis (Concept 3 - Mechanism)

**Placement:** When describing mitochondrial structure and aerobic respiration

**Format:** Cross-section of mitochondrion showing internal structure and electron transport chain

**Visual Description:**
- Outer membrane (permeable, smooth)
- Inner membrane (highly folded into cristae)
- Matrix (the internal space within inner membrane)
- Cristae shown as folded inner membrane
- Electron transport chain proteins (Complexes I-IV) embedded in cristae
- ATP synthase shown as mushroom-shaped protein complex spanning inner membrane
- Proton gradient illustrated: 
  - High concentration of H⁺ in intermembrane space (between membranes)
  - Low concentration in matrix
  - Protons flowing through ATP synthase
- Glucose entering, being broken down (show pyruvate entering via transporter)
- Electrons flowing along transport chain
- Protons being pumped across membrane (using energy from electron transfer)

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Shows the compartmentalization of energy production. Explains ATP synthesis via chemiosmosis. Motivates why cells with high energy demand have many mitochondria.

**Style Notes:** Use shading or color gradient to show proton concentration. Show electron flow with arrows. Include electron carriers (NAD⁺, FADH₂) if space allows. Use realistic proportions of surface area due to cristae.

**Caption:** "The mitochondrion is divided by two membranes. The inner membrane is folded into cristae, creating enormous surface area for the electron transport chain. As nutrients are oxidized, electrons flow through protein complexes in the inner membrane, releasing energy that pumps protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space. Protons accumulate, creating a gradient. As they flow back through ATP synthase, the mechanical energy drives phosphorylation of ADP to ATP. This elegant system converts chemical energy into the cell's energy currency with remarkable efficiency."

### 11. Insulin Secretion Pathway (Concept 3 - Worked Example)

**Placement:** After insulin secretion description

**Format:** Flow diagram or numbered sequence showing insulin's path from gene to secretion

**Visual Description:**
- Step 1: Nucleus with insulin gene active (shown with DNA and transcription machinery)
- Step 2: mRNA exiting nucleus
- Step 3: mRNA at rough ER, ribosome translating, insulin protein being synthesized and fed into ER lumen
- Step 4: Insulin protein with added carbohydrate chains (shown as branching structures)
- Step 5: Insulin packaged into transport vesicle budding from ER
- Step 6: Vesicle fusing with Golgi apparatus (cis-face)
- Step 7: Insulin moving through Golgi cisternae (further modification shown)
- Step 8: Insulin packaged into secretory vesicle at trans-Golgi network
- Step 9: Secretory vesicle transporting to plasma membrane
- Step 10: Vesicle fusing with plasma membrane (exocytosis)
- Step 11: Insulin released into bloodstream

**Pedagogical Purpose:** Integrates transcription, translation, organelle function, and secretion. Shows how the cell executes the program of the genome. Demonstrates practical relevance (insulin is vital hormone).

**Style Notes:** Number each step. Use consistent colors: DNA/RNA in blue, proteins in red/orange, vesicles with borders. Show sizes approximately correctly (mRNA much smaller than vesicle).

**Caption:** "Insulin synthesis and secretion demonstrates the integration of multiple cellular processes. The insulin gene is transcribed in the nucleus, producing mRNA that travels to the rough ER. Ribosomes translate the mRNA, synthesizing insulin, which is immediately modified and packaged. Vesicles carry insulin through the Golgi apparatus for further processing, then transport it to the plasma membrane. Exocytosis releases insulin into the bloodstream. The entire process from gene activation to secretion takes minutes, with trillions of insulin molecules following this pathway simultaneously in active pancreatic tissue."

## Style Consistency Notes

- **Typography:** Chapter titles in sans-serif, bold. Figure titles in italic. Labels in regular sans-serif.
- **Color Palette:** Grayscale or limited colors preferred (supports accessibility). Key distinctions: hydrophobic (tan/neutral), hydrophilic (blue), proteins (orange/red), solutes (purple/teal).
- **Arrow Style:** Black solid arrows for movement, dashed arrows for suppressed or inhibited processes, thick arrows for primary pathway, thin for secondary.
- **Scale Indicators:** Include scale bars or relative size comparisons where relevant (e.g., "~5 nm" for membrane thickness).
- **Labels:** All labels in English, on figure or immediately below. Avoid overly technical jargon in captions; explain terms on first use.

---

## Accessibility Notes

- All figures should have alt-text descriptions for screen readers.
- Color should not be the only distinguishing feature; use patterns (hatching, dotted fills) in addition to color.
- Captions should be substantive and explain not just what is shown, but why it matters pedagogically.
- High-contrast versions of figures recommended for students with color blindness (deuteranopia, protanopia).
