## [FIGURE: Sensory Receptor Types]

**Where in chapter:** Concept 1, Mechanism section (after "Structural Types")

**What it shows:** Three columns showing structural categories of sensory receptors:

1. **Free nerve ending** (left): Simple axon branch terminating in skin or tissue, no surrounding structure. Label: "Free nerve ending (thermoreceptor, nociceptor)"
2. **Encapsulated ending** (middle): Nerve surrounded by layers of connective tissue (show cross-section with concentric rings, like an onion). Label: "Encapsulated ending (Pacinian corpuscle — pressure and vibration)"

**Source:** OpenStax CNX module 46577, Figure 1 (receptor classification by cell type). Adapt to show the three structural categories clearly.

**Usage note:** This figure supports the conceptual point that structure determines function. The encapsulated ending explicitly shows how the capsule modifies the stimulus before it reaches the nerve.

## [FIGURE: The Dorsal Column-Medial Lemniscus Pathway]

**Where in chapter:** Concept 2, Mechanism section (after explaining the dorsal column pathway)

**What it shows:** A full-body diagram (sagittal view) showing the pathway from foot touch receptor → dorsal root ganglion → dorsal column (fasciculus gracilis for lower body, fasciculus cuneatus for upper body) → medulla → nucleus gracilis/cuneatus → decussation → medial lemniscus → thalamus → somatosensory cortex.

**Key labels:**
- Sensory receptor in foot (mechanoreceptor)
- Dorsal root ganglion neuron (first-order)
- Dorsal column in spinal cord (fasciculus gracilis and fasciculus cuneatus, showing somatotopic arrangement: legs medial, arms lateral)
- Medulla (nucleus gracilis and nucleus cuneatus)
- Decussation point (crossing in medulla)
- Medial lemniscus (ascending in brain stem)
- Thalamus (second-order to third-order synapse)
- Postcentral gyrus/somatosensory cortex
- Inset: Sensory homunculus (distorted body map showing lips and fingers enlarged)

**Color coding:** First-order neuron one color, second-order neuron another color, third-order neuron a third color, to show the three-neuron chain.

**Source:** OpenStax CNX module 46557 contains this pathway. Adapt to highlight the precise decussation location (medulla, not spinal cord) and the somatotopic organization at each level.

## [FIGURE: The Spinothalamic Tract Pathway]

**Where in chapter:** Concept 2, Mechanism section (after explaining the spinothalamic pathway)

**What it shows:** A parallel diagram to the dorsal column (same spinal cord cross-section, same body regions) showing pain/temperature pathway:

Skin nociceptor → dorsal root ganglion → dorsal horn → second-order neuron decussates IN THE SPINAL CORD → spinothalamic tract ascending → thalamus → somatosensory cortex.

**Key labels:**
- Nociceptor in skin
- Dorsal root ganglion (first-order)
- Dorsal horn (gray matter, where synapse occurs)
- Second-order neuron decussates *in the spinal cord* (this is the critical difference from dorsal column)
- Spinothalamic tract (tight bundle ascending)
- Thalamus
- Somatosensory cortex

**Color coding:** Same three-neuron chain convention as dorsal column figure, but the crossing happens at a different level—show this with a clear label "Decussation occurs in spinal cord at entry level."

**Comparison feature:** Place dorsal column and spinothalamic tract side by side to highlight:
- Dorsal column: ascends before synapsing; decussates in medulla
- Spinothalamic tract: synapses in dorsal horn; decussates in spinal cord at entry level

**Source:** OpenStax CNX module 46557 contains this. The key teaching point is the *different decussation levels*—make this visually obvious.

## [FIGURE: Brown-Séquard Syndrome — Hemisection Pattern]

**Where in chapter:** Concept 2, Worked Example section

**What it shows:** Transverse (cross-sectional) view of spinal cord at a specific level (e.g., C7), showing damage to the left half of the cord.

**Three panels:**

1. **Normal spinal cord (reference):** Show left and right dorsal columns (carrying fine touch, proprioception) and left and right spinothalamic tracts (carrying pain, temperature). Label intact pathways on both sides.

2. **Spinal cord with hemisection on left side:** Damage to the left dorsal column and left spinothalamic tract. Cloud or shaded region showing the lesion.

3. **Resulting sensory loss:**
   - **Fine touch and proprioception:** Loss on the LEFT side (same side as lesion) because the dorsal column on the left is damaged before it crosses in the medulla.
   - **Pain and temperature:** Loss on the RIGHT side (opposite side from lesion) because the spinothalamic tract decussates in the spinal cord; fibers from the right side have already crossed to the left before the lesion and travel up the left side where they are cut.

**Result summary:** Brown-Séquard syndrome produces a crossed pattern of sensory loss. Make this visually obvious so the reader sees immediately why the pathways matter.

**Source:** Construct from understanding of the two pathways. The clinical pattern reveals the anatomy.

## [FIGURE: Upper vs. Lower Motor Neuron Lesions]

**Where in chapter:** Concept 3, The Contrast in Clinical Presentation section

**What it shows:** Three panels showing the pathway and the effects of lesions at different levels.

1. **Normal pathway:** Motor cortex → corticospinal tract → pyramidal decussation in medulla → spinal cord → lower motor neuron → neuromuscular junction → muscle.

2. **Upper motor neuron lesion (e.g., stroke in left motor cortex):**
   - Damage to left motor cortex
   - Result: right-sided paralysis (crossed because of decussation)
   - Muscle appearance: normal (lower motor neuron is intact)
   - Muscle tone: increased (spasticity)
   - Reflexes: exaggerated (hyperreflexia)
   - Show the pathway with lesion on the left, resulting effects on the right side of body

3. **Lower motor neuron lesion (e.g., damage to spinal cord motor neurons on the left):**
   - Damage to left ventral horn motor neurons
   - Result: left-sided paralysis (ipsilateral, same side as lesion)
   - Muscle appearance: wasted/atrophic (lower motor neuron is damaged)
   - Muscle tone: decreased (flaccidity)
   - Reflexes: diminished or absent (hyporeflexia)
   - Show the pathway with lesion on the left, resulting effects on the left side of body

**Color/shading:** Use consistent color to show where the lesion is and where the effects appear. Make the contralateral vs. ipsilateral distinction obvious.

**Side-by-side comparison:** This figure teaches the clinical diagnostic logic: if a patient has right-sided weakness with spasticity, the lesion is on the left side at the level of the motor cortex (UMN). If a patient has left-sided weakness with flaccidity and atrophy, the lesion is on the left side at the level of the spinal cord motor neurons (LMN).

**Source:** Construct from standard motor pathology. OpenStax CNX module 46574 has some of this; supplement with clinical reasoning.

## [FIGURE: Somatotopic Organization in Motor Cortex]

**Where in chapter:** Concept 3, Mechanism section (when introducing upper motor neurons)

**What it shows:** The motor homunculus—a distorted human figure mapped onto the surface of the primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus). Show the distortion: huge hands and face, tiny trunk and legs.

**Key labels:**
- Precentral gyrus (motor cortex)
- Somatotopic organization: legs medial, head and arms lateral
- Distorted body map showing relative amounts of cortex devoted to each body part
- Note: disproportionate representation of hands (fine motor control requires more cortical territory) and face

**Purpose:** Shows that motor control, like sensation, is somatotopically organized. A lesion affecting a specific part of the motor cortex affects a specific part of the body because of this organization.

**Source:** OpenStax CNX module 46574 contains reference to motor cortex organization. The motor homunculus is a classic teaching image.

## [FIGURE: The Three-Neuron Chain in Sensory Pathways]

**Where in chapter:** Concept 2, Mechanism section (to emphasize the common structure)

**What it shows:** A simplified diagram showing that both dorsal column and spinothalamic pathways follow a three-neuron pattern:

- **First-order neuron:** Cell body in dorsal root ganglion, axon enters spinal cord
- **Second-order neuron:** Cell body in spinal cord (dorsal horn for spinothalamic; medulla for dorsal column), axon crosses midline
- **Third-order neuron:** Cell body in thalamus, axon projects to somatosensory cortex

**Differences highlighted:**
- Where the second neuron is located (spinal cord gray matter vs. medulla)
- Where decussation occurs (spinal cord level vs. medulla)
- What information is carried (pain/temp vs. fine touch/proprioception)

**Purpose:** Emphasizes the underlying organizational principle (three neurons) while drawing attention to the differences that matter clinically.

**Source:** Synthesis from OpenStax modules.

## [FIGURE: Reflex Arc (Hot Stove)]

**Where in chapter:** Chapter Opening and Concept 3, Integration section

**What it shows:** A simplified spinal reflex arc showing:

- Hand touching a hot surface
- Pain receptor (nociceptor) firing in skin
- Sensory neuron axon entering spinal cord through dorsal root
- Synapse in spinal cord between sensory neuron and motor neuron
- Motor neuron axon exiting through ventral root
- Muscle (biceps) contracting, pulling hand away
- *Dotted line* showing the slower pathway up to the brain for conscious perception

**Key point:** The reflex happens before consciousness. The direct spinal synapse allows a response in ~50ms, while conscious perception takes ~200-300ms. This is adaptive.

**Source:** Standard reflex arc diagram. OpenStax CNX module 46561 covers this. Simplify to show only the essential three components (sensory neuron, spinal synapse, motor neuron) to avoid cognitive overload.

## Image Quality Standards

All images should:
- Use clear, consistent color coding (e.g., red for sensory pathways, blue for motor pathways)
- Include labels in clear, readable font
- Show anatomical position (anterior/posterior, left/right) with standard orientation
- Be simple enough to understand at a glance but detailed enough to teach the mechanism
- Include a caption explaining what is shown and why it matters

---

## Integration with Text

Each figure is referenced in the chapter by caption. The chapter does not describe every element of the figure—that would create redundancy. Instead, the text calls attention to the key teaching point, and the figure shows it anatomically. Together, they teach the concept.

Example: The text explains that "the spinothalamic tract decussates in the spinal cord, while the dorsal column decussates in the medulla." The figure shows both pathways side by side, with the decussation points at different anatomical levels, making this difference visually obvious.
