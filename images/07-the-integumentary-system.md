# Images: The Integumentary System

## Figure 1: The Five Layers of the Epidermis (Cross-section)

**Placement:** Concept 1, immediately after the mechanism section begins. This is the foundational figure; without it, the reader is navigating by description alone.

**Description:** A vertical cross-section showing the epidermis from stratum corneum (top) to stratum basale (bottom). Each layer should be visibly distinct in color or texture. Stratum corneum: dead, flat cells, light color. Stratum lucidum (if included): translucent, barely visible except on thick skin. Stratum granulosum: cells with visible granules, transitional appearance. Stratum spinosum: cells with visible desmosomal connections (spiky appearance, though note that this is partly an artifact). Stratum basale: single row of cuboidal cells, some dividing (indicated by a nucleus visible, a metaphase spindle, or a cell labeled as "basal cell").

**Pedagogical purpose:** Establishes the spatial organization of the layers. Helps the reader see the progression from living to dead, from cubic to flat, from nucleus to no nucleus. The figure should make it visually clear why cells in the stratum corneum are dead (no nucleus visible) and why cells in the stratum basale are alive (nucleus visible, actively dividing).

**Cell types to label:** Basal cells, melanocytes, Merkel cells, Langerhans cells (in stratum spinosum).

**Annotations:** Arrow showing the direction of cell movement (upward, from stratum basale toward stratum corneum). Label indicating "4-week replacement cycle."

**Style notes:** Use distinct colors for each layer to reinforce that they are distinct zones. The cells should be drawn to scale with each other—basal cells are cuboidal, spinosum cells are polyhedral, granulosum cells are flattened, corneum cells are extremely flat. Use arrows or shading to indicate the progression.

## Figure 2: The Epidermis and Dermis Together (Higher magnification)

**Placement:** Early in Concept 2, to show how the epidermis sits on the dermis and how dermal papillae interlock with epidermal layers.

**Description:** A cross-section showing the epidermis (now simplified into fewer visible layers for clarity) sitting on top of the dermis. The dermal papillae should be visible as finger-like projections from the dermis that push up into the stratum basale. The basement membrane should be drawn as a distinct line between epidermis and dermis.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Helps the reader understand how the two layers are connected (via the basement membrane and dermal papillae), and that the papillae increase surface contact between the two layers. This sets up the discussion of fingerprints (where ridges form at the interface between papillae and basal cells).

**Details to label:** Basement membrane, dermal papilla, stratum basale, dermis.

**Optional details:** Blood capillaries within the papilla. Sensory receptors (Meissner corpuscle) in the papilla.

**Style notes:** The dermal papillae should be drawn as prominent and numerous, to make it clear that the interface between epidermis and dermis is not flat but highly folded.

## Figure 3: The Dermis (Two layers, vascular and neural detail)

**Placement:** Mid-Concept 2, after the mechanism section. This figure shows the vascular and nervous system detail of the dermis.

**Description:** A cross-section of the dermis divided into two layers. Papillary layer (upper): loose areolar tissue, with blood capillaries, Meissner corpuscles (touch receptors), and phagocytes visible. Reticular layer (lower): dense tissue with larger blood vessels, Pacinian corpuscles (pressure/vibration receptors), temperature and pain receptors (indicated by nerve endings).

**Pedagogical purpose:** Establishes that the dermis is not just structural but actively vascular and neural. The figure should make it clear that sensation is distributed throughout the dermis, not just at the top. It should show that blood vessels are abundant.

**Details to label:** Papillary layer, reticular layer, blood capillaries, arterioles/venules, Meissner corpuscles, Pacinian corpuscles, nerve fibers, collagen fibers.

**Collagen visualization:** Use line drawing or shading to show collagen fibers as a dense mesh. In the papillary layer, the mesh should be loose; in the reticular layer, dense.

**Style notes:** Use color or shading to distinguish the two layers. The blood vessels should be drawn in red; nerves in yellow or orange. Receptors should be labeled and color-coded by type.

## Figure 4: Hair Follicle Anatomy (Longitudinal cross-section)

**Placement:** Early in Concept 3, before the detailed description of hair structure.

**Description:** A vertical cross-section of a hair follicle extending from the skin surface down into the dermis. The hair shaft (visible at the surface) should be drawn as dead, keratinized, continuous with the deeper parts. Below the surface, the hair root should be shown, surrounded by the internal root sheath and external root sheath. At the base, the hair bulb should be shown, with the hair matrix (site of cell division) inside the bulb. The hair papilla (connective tissue with blood capillaries and nerve endings) should be shown at the very base, inside the bulb.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Establishes that hair grows from the matrix (at the base), not from the shaft visible on the skin. The figure should make clear that the shaft is dead and cannot sense anything, but that the matrix is alive and dividing.

**Details to label:** Hair shaft, hair root, hair bulb, hair matrix, hair papilla, internal root sheath, external root sheath, arrector pili muscle, sebaceous gland duct (entering the follicle).

**Color coding:** The hair shaft should be one color (dead, keratinized). The matrix should be another (living, dividing). The papilla should be colored to show blood capillaries (red).

**Optional:** Include a small figure showing the three phases of hair growth (anagen, catagen, telogen).

**Style notes:** Make the distinction between the dead shaft and the living matrix very clear. The matrix should look active (cells dividing); the shaft should look inert.

## Figure 5: Sweat Gland Types (Two side-by-side cross-sections)

**Placement:** Early in Concept 3, in the sweat glands section.

**Description:** Two parallel cross-sections, one showing an eccrine gland, one showing an apocrine gland. Both should show the gland extending from the skin surface down into the dermis or hypodermis, with a duct running up to a pore on the surface.

Eccrine gland: should show the coiled secretory unit deep in the dermis, with a relatively narrow duct rising to the skin surface. Label the sweat it produces as "watery, hypotonic" or "mostly water."

Apocrine gland: should show a larger gland (often at the base of a hair follicle), with a larger duct that may empty into the hair follicle rather than directly onto the skin surface. Label the sweat as "thicker, contains organic compounds."

**Pedagogical purpose:** Establishes the anatomical differences between the two gland types and helps the reader understand why apocrine glands are associated with odor (the bacteria-friendly organic compounds) while eccrine glands are purely thermoregulatory.

**Placement emphasis:** Note that eccrine glands are all over the surface, while apocrine glands are concentrated in the armpits and genital regions.

**Style notes:** Use different colors to distinguish the two gland types. The secretory units should be drawn to show the difference in depth and size.

## Figure 6: Nail Anatomy (Cross-section and side view)

**Placement:** Mid-Concept 3, in the nails section.

**Description:** Two views: (1) cross-section showing the nail body from above, with labels for the nail body, lateral nail folds, nail cuticle (eponychium), free edge. (2) Side view showing the depth of the nail bed, the nail root extending into the epidermis, the lunula (crescent-shaped region at the base), and the matrix (the source of cell growth).

**Pedagogical purpose:** Helps the reader understand that nails are not just surface structures but are rooted in the epidermis. The lunula visible on the nail surface is the place where the matrix (which is beneath the nail, too deep to see) is making new cells.

**Details to label:** Nail body, nail root, nail matrix, nail cuticle (eponychium), nail folds, lunula, hyponychium, nail bed.

**Vascular details:** Show blood vessels in the nail bed (which makes it appear pink) and show that the lunula appears whiter because of the thicker epithelium over the matrix.

**Growth arrow:** Include an arrow showing the direction of nail growth (outward from the root, toward the free edge).

**Style notes:** The color of the nail should reflect that it is living (pink) in the nail bed, and white or pale at the lunula and free edge (where it is growing or has grown out). Use shading to show depth.

## Figure 7: Temperature Regulation via Skin (Two state views)

**Placement:** In Concept 2, in the "blood vessels serve multiple functions" paragraph, or in the Integration section.

**Description:** Two side-by-side cross-sections of the same skin region under different temperature conditions.

State 1 (Cold): Arterioles in the dermis are constricted. Blood flow to the skin is minimal. The skin appears pale. Label: "Thermoregulation: heat conservation."

State 2 (Hot): Arterioles in the dermis are dilated. Blood flow to the skin is maximal. The skin appears flushed or red. Label: "Thermoregulation: heat dissipation."

Additionally, in state 2, sweat glands could be shown as active (with sweat visible on the skin surface).

**Pedagogical purpose:** Shows that thermoregulation is not a passive property of the skin but an active process involving nervous system signals and changes in blood vessel diameter. The figure should make it visually clear that the same skin structure can respond in opposite ways depending on the signal from the sympathetic nervous system.

**Style notes:** Use color to show blood flow (more red in hot state, less in cold state). The arterioles should be visibly wider in the hot state and narrower in the cold state.

## Figure 8: The Melanin Protection-Vitamin D Trade-off

**Placement:** In Concept 1, in the "Common misconceptions" section on melanin, or in the pantry.

**Description:** A diagram (not a cross-section) showing the balance between melanin protection and vitamin D synthesis. One side of the balance: more melanin = better UV protection, less skin cancer. Other side: more melanin = less vitamin D synthesis, greater need for sun exposure.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Makes the trade-off visible as an actual balance, not just a verbal statement. Helps the reader understand that there is no "optimal" level of melanin—the optimal level depends on the individual's environment (how much sun exposure is available) and needs (need for vitamin D vs. risk of skin cancer).

**Variations:** Could show specific skin color categories (light, medium, dark) and the typical vitamin D synthesis and cancer risk for each, but this risks oversimplifying and essentializing skin color. Better to show it as a continuous trade-off.

**Style notes:** Use a balance scale image, with one side labeled "UV Protection" and the other "Vitamin D Synthesis." Show melanin level on the fulcrum.

## Figure 9: Wound Healing Stages (Four-panel progression)

**Placement:** In the Integration section or as a summary figure.

**Description:** A four-panel progression showing the stages of wound healing from a cut.

Panel 1 (Immediate): Blood clot forming to stop bleeding. Label: "Hemostasis and inflammation."

Panel 2 (Hours to days): Fibroblasts migrating into the wound site. Inflammatory cells (macrophages) present. Label: "Fibroblast mobilization and immune response."

Panel 3 (Days to weeks): Collagen deposition, granulation tissue forming. New blood capillaries growing into the area. Label: "Collagen deposition and revascularization."

Panel 4 (Weeks to months): The wound is filled with collagen (scar tissue). The scar is lighter or lighter than surrounding skin. Label: "Scar maturation."

**Pedagogical purpose:** Shows that wound healing is not instantaneous but a process with distinct stages. Each stage involves different cell types and processes. The figure helps the reader understand why deep wounds scar: the body fills the gap with collagen, which is structurally different from normal skin.

**Style notes:** Use progression of colors to show the stages (red in inflammation stage, white in collagen deposition stage, pale in scar maturation stage). Include labels for the major cell types and processes at each stage.

## Figure 10: Burn Classification (Depth illustration)

**Placement:** In the Concept 2 case (blister) or in a supplementary section on burns.

**Description:** A series of cross-sections showing burns of increasing severity:

First-degree: epidermis only, light color, intact dermis below.
Second-degree: epidermis and upper dermis, showing the blister forming at the interface, blood vessels visible, blistering fluid shown.
Third-degree: full thickness, epidermis and dermis completely destroyed, damage extending into the hypodermis or beyond, charred appearance.
Fourth-degree: epidermis, dermis, and muscle/bone involvement.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Helps the reader understand why deeper burns are more serious. First-degree burns heal on their own; deeper burns require intervention (fluid replacement, infection prevention, grafting). The figure makes the depth of damage visible.

**Labels:** Include "Stratum corneum" and show progressively how much deeper each burn extends.

**Style notes:** Use color and shading to show the extent of damage at each level. Dead or damaged tissue should be colored differently (gray, black) than live tissue (pink, red).
