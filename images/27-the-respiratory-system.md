## Figure 1: Bronchial Tree with Generation Labeling

**Caption:** Bronchial tree from trachea to alveoli, showing progressive branching and diameter reduction. Each generation roughly doubles the number of tubes while halving the diameter. By generation 23, approximately 500 million alveoli are reached.

**Elements to include:**
- Trachea (diameter ~18 mm)
- Primary bronchi (diameter ~11 mm)
- Secondary and tertiary bronchi (smaller diameters, labeled)
- Bronchioles (diameter <1 mm)
- Terminal bronchioles (diameter ~0.5 mm)
- Respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts
- Cluster of alveoli (enlarged inset showing individual alveoli, ~200 micrometers diameter)

**Labels:**
- Generation number (1, 5, 10, 15, 20, 23)
- Diameter at each level
- Number of branches at each level (exponential growth)
- Conducting zone vs. respiratory zone (color coding or line)

**Physiological note:** The exponential branching ensures that the total cross-sectional area increases as tubes branch and narrow, keeping resistance manageable while maximizing surface area.

## Figure 2: Cross-Section of Alveolus with Cell Types

**Caption:** Histological detail of a single alveolus showing the three cell types of the alveolar wall and the capillary network.

**Elements to include:**
- **Type I alveolar cells**: Large, flat, squamous epithelial cells (~25 nm thick) covering ~97% of the alveolar surface area. Color code lightly (e.g., pale blue).
- **Type II alveolar cells**: Cuboidal, interspersed among type I cells. Show rough endoplasmic reticulum and lamellar bodies (storage of surfactant). Color code distinctly (e.g., tan/cream).
- **Alveolar macrophage**: Dendritic cell shape, shown within the alveolar space or between epithelial cells, armed with pseudopodia. Color code (e.g., orange).
- **Alveolar basement membrane**: Thin line separating the alveolar epithelium from the capillary endothelium.
- **Capillary endothelium**: Single layer of cells, pressed directly against the alveolar wall across the respiratory membrane.
- **Red blood cell**: Within the capillary lumen, shown to scale (~7 micrometers diameter) and larger than the capillary diameter to show that RBCs deform as they pass through.
- **Pores of Kohn** (alveolar pores): Small openings in the alveolar wall connecting neighboring alveoli. Show a few on the diagram.

**Scale and labels:**
- Respiratory membrane thickness: 0.5 micrometers (marked)
- Alveolar diameter: ~200 micrometers (marked)
- RBC diameter: ~7 micrometers (marked)

**Arrows and annotations:**
- Arrow showing O₂ diffusion from alveolar space into RBC
- Arrow showing CO₂ diffusion from RBC into alveolar space
- Label indicating "Simple squamous epithelium" (the type I cells)
- Label indicating "Tight junction" or note that type I cells are not directly joined in some regions (water and gases can pass through paracellular pathways)

**Elements to include:**

**Labels and annotations:**

- Intrapleural pressure (–4 mm Hg relative to atmospheric, approximately constant)

- Arrows showing the direction of diaphragm movement
- Arrows showing rib cage expansion

**Color coding suggestion:**
- Rib cage: light gray
- Lung tissue: pink (with bronchioles visible)
- Pleural fluid: pale blue

## Figure 4: Partial Pressures at Sea Level

**Caption:** Partial pressures of O₂ and CO₂ at different points in the respiratory and circulatory pathways at sea level.

**Elements to include:**

A linear diagram showing a path: **Atmosphere → Alveolus → Arterial Blood → Tissue → Venous Blood → Alveolus → Exhaled Air**

**For each location, show:**
- O₂ partial pressure (mm Hg)
- CO₂ partial pressure (mm Hg)

**Specific values:**
- Atmosphere: PO₂ = 159 mm Hg; PCO₂ = 0.3 mm Hg
- Alveolus: PO₂ = 104 mm Hg; PCO₂ = 40 mm Hg
- Arterial blood (leaving lungs): PO₂ = 100 mm Hg; PCO₂ = 40 mm Hg
- Tissue (resting): PO₂ = 40 mm Hg; PCO₂ = 45 mm Hg
- Venous blood (returning to lungs): PO₂ = 40 mm Hg; PCO₂ = 45 mm Hg
- Exhaled air: PO₂ = 116 mm Hg (mix of alveolar and dead space air); PCO₂ = 32 mm Hg

**Visual encoding:**
- Use a color gradient (blue for low PO₂, red for high; opposite for PCO₂)
- Or use bar graphs at each location
- Show gradients with arrows indicating the direction of gas movement

**Annotation:**
- Highlight the large O₂ gradient from alveolus (104) to venous blood (40) as the driving force for external respiration
- Highlight the small CO₂ gradient from tissue (45) to venous blood (45)—almost no gradient! Yet CO₂ exits because it is more soluble and there are other transport mechanisms

## Figure 5: The Three Mechanisms of CO₂ Transport

**Caption:** Three parallel pathways for CO₂ transport from tissue to lungs.

**Design:** Three side-by-side panels, each showing one mechanism.

**Panel A: Dissolved CO₂ (7–10%)**
- Tissue cell releasing CO₂ (arrow)
- CO₂ molecules shown as small spheres entering plasma
- CO₂ traveling through plasma to the lung
- CO₂ diffusing across the respiratory membrane into the alveolus
- Label: "Dissolved in Plasma; Simple Diffusion"
- Percentage contribution: "~8%"

**Panel B: Bicarbonate Buffer (70%)**
- Tissue cell releasing CO₂
- CO₂ entering a red blood cell
- Inside the RBC: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (with carbonic anhydrase enzyme shown as a protein shape)
- H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
- HCO₃⁻ accumulating in the RBC and then exiting via transporter protein
- Cl⁻ entering the RBC (chloride shift)
- HCO₃⁻ traveling in plasma to the lung
- Inside the lung's RBC: reverse reaction
- CO₂ diffusing out
- Label: "Bicarbonate Buffer System"
- Percentage contribution: "~70%"
- Show the enzyme carbonic anhydrase as a red protein shape

**Panel C: Carbaminohemoglobin (20%)**
- Red blood cells shown in tissue
- Hemoglobin releasing O₂ (becoming deoxygenated; color change from bright red to darker)
- CO₂ binding to the globin chains of hemoglobin (not to heme iron)
- Carbaminohemoglobin traveling to the lung
- In the lung, O₂ rebinding, CO₂ releasing
- Label: "Carbaminohemoglobin; Haldane Effect"
- Percentage contribution: "~20%"

**Unified annotation:**
- Total CO₂ transported: 100%
- Show the sum: 8 + 70 + 20 = 98% (rounding and precision note)

## Figure 6: Oxygen-Hemoglobin Dissociation Curve

**Caption:** The sigmoid (S-shaped) oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve, showing saturation as a function of partial pressure of oxygen. Effects of pH (Bohr effect), temperature, and 2,3-BPG are shown as curve shifts.

**Main curve:**
- X-axis: Partial pressure of O₂ (0–100 mm Hg)
- Y-axis: Hemoglobin saturation (0–100%)
- Main curve: sigmoid shape, steep between 20–60 mm Hg (physiological range)
- Mark key points:
  - At PO₂ = 100 mm Hg (arterial, lungs): saturation ≈ 97.5%
  - At PO₂ = 40 mm Hg (venous, resting tissue): saturation ≈ 75%
  - At PO₂ = 20 mm Hg (active tissue): saturation ≈ 30%

**Shifted curves (shown as lighter/colored variants):**
- **Right shift (Bohr effect, decreased pH or increased temperature or increased BPG)**: Curve shifts to the right, meaning at any given PO₂, saturation is lower. This promotes oxygen unloading in tissue.
- **Left shift (increased pH, decreased temperature, decreased BPG)**: Curve shifts to the left, meaning at any given PO₂, saturation is higher. This promotes oxygen loading in the lungs.

**Label the factors:**
- Decreased pH → right shift (lower pH promotes oxygen release at the tissue)
- Increased temperature → right shift
- Increased 2,3-BPG → right shift
- Increased pH → left shift
- Decreased temperature → left shift
- Decreased 2,3-BPG → left shift

**Physiological significance annotation:**
- "The steep middle portion ensures responsive O₂ delivery to active tissues"
- "Cooperative binding of O₂ to hemoglobin subunits creates the sigmoidal shape"

## Figure 7: Ventilation-Perfusion Matching

**Caption:** Three alveoli showing matched, underperfused, and unventilated states, with the body's compensatory responses.

**Three scenarios:**

**Left (Matched/Normal):**
- Alveolus with good ventilation (air flow in)
- Capillary with good blood flow (red cells flowing)
- High PO₂ in alveolus (~104 mm Hg)
- High PO₂ in capillary blood (~100 mm Hg)
- Efficient gas exchange
- Label: "Optimal V/Q Match"

**Center (Underperfused/High Ventilation-Perfusion Ratio):**
- Alveolus with ventilation (air flow in)
- Capillary with reduced blood flow (fewer red cells)
- High PO₂ in alveolus (air accumulates)
- Low blood flow means oxygen is not being carried away
- Body response: bronchiole constricts (reduce ventilation) or arteriole dilates (increase perfusion)
- Label: "V/Q > 1 (Ventilation > Perfusion)"

**Right (Unventilated/High Perfusion-Ventilation Ratio):**
- Alveolus with poor ventilation (blocked bronchiole or mucus)
- Capillary with good blood flow (red cells flowing but no oxygenation)
- Low PO₂ in alveolus (not renewed)
- Blood arrives expecting oxygen but alveolus is hypoxic
- Body response: arteriole constricts (redirect blood to better-ventilated alveoli)
- Label: "V/Q < 1 (Perfusion > Ventilation)"

**Mechanisms shown:**
- In the underperfused case: show vasodilation (arteriole widening) or bronchiole constriction
- In the unventilated case: show vasoconstriction (arteriole narrowing)
- Arrows indicating blood flow redistribution

## Figure 8: Altitude Effects on Oxygen Partial Pressure

**Caption:** Comparison of atmospheric, alveolar, and arterial oxygen partial pressures at sea level, 5,000 feet, and 20,000 feet (Mt. Everest).

**Three columns (sea level, 5,000 feet, 20,000 feet):**

For each altitude, show:
- Atmospheric pressure (mm Hg)
- Atmospheric PO₂ (mm Hg)
- Alveolar PO₂ (mm Hg)
- Arterial PO₂ (mm Hg)
- Hemoglobin saturation (%)

**Specific values:**
- Sea level: 760 atm → 159 PO₂ atm → 104 PO₂ alv → 100 PO₂ art → 97% sat
- 5,000 feet: 632 atm → 133 PO₂ atm → 85 PO₂ alv → 80 PO₂ art → 88% sat
- 20,000 feet: 350 atm → 73 PO₂ atm → 54 PO₂ alv → 50 PO₂ art → 76% sat

**Visual encoding:**
- Use bars or pie charts to show the progression
- Color gradient showing oxygen availability (high = bright red/orange, low = dark red)
- Annotations highlighting the point: "At Everest, hemoglobin saturation drops to 76%, despite 21% oxygen in the atmosphere"

**Physiological note:**
- Show how the body compensates: increased ventilation, increased RBC production, increased BPG
- Note that despite the low saturation, resting tissues still receive adequate oxygen because a greater *proportion* of oxygen is released at high altitude

## Figure 9: Hemoglobin Structure and Oxygen Binding

**Caption:** Quaternary structure of hemoglobin showing four subunits, each with a heme group (iron-containing), and the conformational change upon oxygen binding.

**Left (Deoxy/T-state):**
- Four subunits arranged in a roughly tetrahedral shape
- Each subunit shown with a heme (iron-containing ring) in the center
- Subunits shown in a relaxed, looser conformation
- Label: "T-state (Tense): Low oxygen affinity"

**Right (Oxy/R-state):**
- Same four subunits
- All four hemes bound to oxygen (shown as O₂ molecules attached to iron)
- Subunits shown in a tighter, more compact conformation
- Label: "R-state (Relaxed): High oxygen affinity"

**Annotation of cooperativity:**
- Arrow showing the conformational change from T to R
- Note: "Binding of first O₂ favors binding of subsequent O₂ molecules (positive cooperativity)"
- Show stepwise binding: Hb → HbO₂ → Hb(O₂)₂ → Hb(O₂)₃ → Hb(O₂)₄

**Color coding:**
- Iron atom: shown as a central red dot
- Deoxygenated subunits: darker color
- Oxygenated subunits: bright red

## Technical Notes

### Resolution and Labeling
- All figures should be high-resolution (vector-based preferred for clarity, or high DPI raster)
- Anatomical labels should use standard terminology (given in the chapter)
- Font size should be readable on both printed page and screen (minimum 10–12 pt for labels)

### Color Coding
- Use consistent color schemes across figures:
  - Oxygen-rich: bright red
  - Oxygen-poor: dark red/burgundy
  - Neutral tissue/structure: gray or pale color
  - CO₂ or high acid: light yellow or orange
  - Active processes or enzymes: contrasting colors (red, blue, orange)

### Scale Bars and Measurements
- Include scale bars (micrometers, millimeters, centimeters) where appropriate
- Label actual dimensions (e.g., "~200 micrometers diameter")
- Use scientific notation where values are very large or very small

### Arrows and Flow
- Indicate direction of air flow, blood flow, and gas diffusion with arrows
- Use arrowhead style consistently (e.g., simple triangles for all flow arrows)
- For gradients, consider gradient-colored arrows (blue to red for oxygen concentration)

### Integration with Text
- Each figure should be referenced in the chapter by number and caption
- Captions should be standalone—readable without referring to the text
- Use consistent terminology between figures and chapter text
