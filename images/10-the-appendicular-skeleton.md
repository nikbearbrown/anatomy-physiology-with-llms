# Images and Figures: Ch. 10 — The Appendicular Skeleton

Recommended illustrations, figure placement, and pedagogical notes for diagrams used in the Attenborough × Feynman v1.1 chapter.

---

## Figure 1: Chapter Opener — Dancer in Extension

**Placement:** Top of chapter (after TL;DR, before chapter opening section)

**Description:** A figure dancer in mid-arabesque—extended arm, curved leg, held position. The skeleton is visible as an overlay (semi-transparent bone) showing the shoulder joint angle, spine extension, and hip joint alignment.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Visual anchor for the opening narrative. Shows what the mobile shoulder and constrained hip make possible. Sets up the contrast immediately.

**Style notes:** Photograph or illustration, color or line art acceptable. The point is clarity, not artistic flourish. The skeleton overlay must be anatomically accurate and readable.

**Source precedent:** Similar to anatomical overlay figures in Anatomy for Artists texts (e.g., Sarah Simblet's *Anatomy for the Artist*).

## Figure 2: Axial vs. Appendicular Skeleton

**Placement:** Chapter opening, after the constraint section

**Description:** Full skeleton with the axial skeleton highlighted in one color (e.g., gray) and the appendicular skeleton in another (e.g., blue). The pectoral girdles attach at the sternoclavicular joints. The pelvic girdle attaches at the sacroiliac joints.

**Pedagogical purpose:** Visual confirmation of the two-system division. Emphasizes that these are distinct bone systems with distinct attachment points.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** Yes. Every bone must be in the correct position. No schematic simplification.

**Size:** Should occupy roughly a quarter-page. Students need to see the fine detail—the small sternoclavicular joints, the robust sacroiliac joints.

## Figure 3: Pectoral Girdle — Anterior and Posterior Views

**Placement:** Concept 1 section, after the mechanism subsection

**Description:** Two views (anterior and posterior) of the pectoral girdle in isolation. Labels:
- Clavicle (entire bone, with medial and lateral ends marked)
- Scapula (with labeled landmarks: acromion, spine, coracoid process, glenoid cavity, inferior angle, superior angle)
- Sternum (partial, showing manubrium and articulation point)

**Pedagogical purpose:** Allows students to see the 3D geometry of the girdle. The anterior view shows the clavicle as a horizontal strut. The posterior view shows the scapula's depth and the position of fossae.

**Style notes:** Clinical anatomical illustration style (line art with minimal shading for depth). Color-code ligaments separately if space permits.

**What NOT to include:** Detailed muscle attachments (too cluttered). Nerve and vessel paths (belongs in a physiology chapter, not here).

## Figure 4: The Shoulder Joint — Mobility vs. Stability Comparison

**Placement:** Concept 1, in the trade-off section

**Description:** Cross-sectional views of the glenohumeral (shoulder) joint and the hip joint side-by-side.

For the shoulder:
- Shallow glenoid cavity
- Large humeral head relative to socket depth
- Minimal articular cartilage thickness
- Rotator cuff muscles labeled

For the hip:
- Deep acetabulum
- Femoral head well-seated in socket
- Thicker articular cartilage
- Labrum (cartilage rim) around acetabulum

**Pedagogical purpose:** Visual demonstration of the mechanical difference between shallow and deep sockets. Makes clear why the shoulder is mobile and the hip is not.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** Yes. Socket depth and head size must be to scale.

**Style notes:** Color-code: bone gray, cartilage blue, muscles red, ligaments yellow.

## Figure 5: The Pelvic Girdle — Superior View

**Placement:** Concept 2 section, after the mechanism subsection

**Description:** View from above (looking down at the pelvis). Shows:
- Both hip bones (left and right)
- Sacrum and coccyx in the center
- Pubic symphysis anteriorly (where the two pubic bones meet)
- Sacroiliac joints (posteriorly)
- Major bony landmarks: anterior superior iliac spines, ischial tuberosities, acetabula

**Pedagogical purpose:** Shows the ring structure of the pelvis. Students can see how weight travels from one leg, through the sacroiliac joint, to the sacrum, and down the opposite leg. The closed ring is the key to understanding load transfer.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** High. Proportions and angles must be correct.

**Style notes:** Use different colors for different bones (left hip bone one color, right another, sacrum a third) to make the structure clear. Posterior view might also be useful to show how the sacrum sits between the hip bones.

## Figure 6: Female vs. Male Pelvis — Structural Comparison

**Placement:** Concept 2, in the trade-off section

**Description:** Two full pelvises (one female, one male) shown in the same orientation (anterior or superior view). Highlight differences:
- Pelvic inlet shape (oval vs. heart-shaped)
- Subpubic angle (>80° vs. <70°)
- Width at the ischial tuberosities (wider vs. narrower)
- Sacral shape (shorter, wider vs. longer, narrower)

**Pedagogical purpose:** Makes the sex differences visible. Students can see immediately why the female pelvis is wider and understand the functional consequence.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** Critical. These structural differences are real and measurable.

**Style notes:** Use a neutral color for both. Overlay a scale or measurement lines showing the different angles and widths.

**Clinical note:** Indicate that pelvic shape is the most reliable skeletal feature for determining biological sex in forensic anthropology.

## Figure 7: Upper Limb Bones — Anterior and Posterior Views

**Placement:** Concept 3 section, after the humerus mechanism description

**Description:** Full upper limb skeleton (humerus, radius, ulna, carpal bones, metacarpals, phalanges) shown anterior and posterior. Labels for major landmarks:

**Humerus:**
- Head (articulating surface)
- Greater tubercle, lesser tubercle
- Intertubercular (bicipital) groove
- Deltoid tuberosity
- Medial and lateral epicondyles
- Trochlea, capitulum

**Radius and ulna:**
- Radial head
- Radial tuberosity
- Ulnar olecranon process
- Radial and ulnar styloid processes

**Hand:**
- Carpal bones (arranged in two rows)
- Metacarpals
- Phalanges

**Pedagogical purpose:** Allows students to see all the articulation points and muscle attachment sites at once. The anterior and posterior views show different landmarks and the overall geometry.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** High. Every bone, every landmark.

**Style notes:** Use shading to show the slight anterior curve of the humeral shaft. Different colors for radius (medial, thumb side) and ulna (lateral) for clarity.

## Figure 8: Lower Limb Bones — Anterior and Posterior Views

**Placement:** Concept 3 section, after the femur mechanism description

**Description:** Full lower limb skeleton (femur, patella, tibia, fibula, tarsals, metatarsals, phalanges) shown anterior and posterior. Labels for:

**Femur:**
- Femoral head (articulating surface)
- Fovea capitis
- Greater trochanter, lesser trochanter
- Intertrochanteric line (anterior), intertrochanteric crest (posterior)
- Linea aspera (posterior shaft)
- Medial and lateral condyles
- Medial and lateral epicondyles
- Adductor tubercle
- Patellar surface

**Tibia and fibula:**
- Tibial condyles
- Tibial tuberosity
- Anterior border (subcutaneous)
- Medial and lateral malleoli

**Foot:**
- Tarsals, metatarsals, phalanges

**Pedagogical purpose:** Students see the overall architecture of the weight-bearing limb. The thickness of the femur (relative to the humerus) should be visually obvious. The posterior view shows the linea aspera and muscle attachment areas.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** High. Proportions matter—students should see that the femur is much thicker than the humerus.

**Style notes:** Use shading to emphasize the thickness of the femoral shaft. Show the curvature of the femur (slight anterior bow). Use different colors for tibia (medial, weight-bearing) and fibula (lateral, non-weight-bearing) for clarity.

## Figure 9: Humerus vs. Femur — Direct Comparison

**Placement:** Concept 3, in the worked example or trade-off section

**Description:** Humerus and femur placed side-by-side at the same scale. Show:
- Relative thickness of the shafts
- Relative size of the proximal ends
- Relative size of the distal articulating surfaces
- Muscle attachment areas (roughened regions)

**Pedagogical purpose:** Makes visceral the difference in bone design for different loads. Students see that the femur is much thicker—and understand why.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** Must be to scale. If they are the same length on the page (which they are not in reality), the femur will still appear substantially thicker.

**Style notes:** Use measurement calipers or dimension lines to show cross-sectional diameter differences.

## Figure 10: The Foot Arches — Three Arches Illustrated

**Placement:** Concept 3, in the integration or synthesis section

**Description:** Medial view of the right foot showing:
- The medial longitudinal arch (highest arc)
- The lateral longitudinal arch (lower, flatter arc)
- The transverse arch (curve across the foot, perpendicular to the longitudinal arches)

Label the bones that form each arch. Show the talus at the peak of the medial arch (the highest point where weight is received from the tibia).

**Pedagogical purpose:** Visual representation of the shock-absorbing structure of the foot. Students can see how the arches flatten under load and rebound.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** High. The arch heights must be realistic.

**Style notes:** Use a lighter color for the bones and a darker color for the ligaments that support the arches. Indicate with arrows the direction of weight transfer (from talus posteriorly to calcaneus and anteriorly through the metatarsal heads).

## Figure 11: Force Transfer Through the Lower Limb (Optional Advanced Figure)

**Placement:** Integration section or appendix

**Description:** Schematic diagram showing the path of weight through the lower limb during single-leg stance:
- Weight travels from the trunk to the femur head
- Through the femur shaft to the knee
- Through the tibia to the ankle
- Through the foot arches to the ground

Use arrows to show direction of force. Use color intensity to show magnitude of force (darker = greater force).

**Pedagogical purpose:** Helps students understand the load path through the skeleton. Can discuss how injuries at any point in the chain affect the whole system.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** Moderate. This is schematic, not anatomically detailed, but the general path must be correct.

**Style notes:** Simplified, schematic representation. Not meant to show every bone and ligament, just the main load path.

## Figure 12: Fracture Sites and Vulnerability (Clinical Illustration)

**Placement:** Common misconceptions section or clinical sidebar

**Description:** Illustration of bones with marked vulnerability zones:
- Femoral neck (narrow point, stress concentration)
- Radial styloid process (common scaphoid fracture site)
- Distal radius (Colles fracture site)
- Clavicle (most common fracture site for this bone)

For each, indicate why that zone is vulnerable (narrow cross-section, stress concentration, impact zone).

**Pedagogical purpose:** Connects anatomy to clinical reality. Shows students where bones actually break and why.

**Anatomical accuracy needed:** High. Fracture sites must be correctly located.

**Style notes:** Use a bright color (red or orange) to highlight vulnerability zones. Include a small inset showing cross-sectional diameter at the fracture site vs. nearby bone.

## General Figure Style Notes

- **Consistency:** All figures should use the same color scheme (bone gray, cartilage blue, muscle red, ligaments yellow) throughout the chapter.
- **Labels:** Clear, legible labels with leader lines (not labels floating over the image).
- **Scale:** Indicate relative scale when comparing bones (e.g., humerus vs. femur).
- **Anterior/Posterior:** Clearly indicate anatomical direction on each figure.
- **Laterality:** Indicate which side (left/right) is shown, especially for paired bones.
- **No oversimplification:** These are anatomy illustrations for a college-level text, not introductory school materials. Anatomical accuracy is expected.
