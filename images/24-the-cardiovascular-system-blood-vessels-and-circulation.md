## Figure 1: Cross-Sectional Anatomy of Arterial and Venous Walls

**What to show:** Four panels arranged in a 2×2 grid.

- **Panel A (top left):** Low-power view of an artery in cross-section. Lumen appears round. Wall appears thick. Three layers are visible: thin inner lining, thick muscular middle layer with smooth muscle bundles (shown in color, e.g., magenta), thick outer connective tissue layer.

- **Panel B (top right):** Low-power view of a vein in cross-section. Lumen appears flattened or irregular. Wall appears thin. Three layers are visible: inner lining similar to artery, thin muscular middle layer, thick outer layer (thicker than middle).

- **Panel C (bottom left):** High-power schematic of artery wall. Label the three tunics:
  - **Tunica intima:** Endothelium (single layer of squamous cells), basement membrane, internal elastic membrane (shown as a dashed yellow line).
  - **Tunica media:** Smooth muscle cells (shown as circular cross-sections), elastic fibers (wavy lines), external elastic membrane (dashed yellow line).
  - **Tunica externa:** Collagenous fibers (wavy), elastic fibers, vasa vasorum (tiny vessels), nervi vasorum (tiny nerve fibers).

- **Panel D (bottom right):** High-power schematic of vein wall. Label the three tunics with same level of detail. Note the differences: thinner tunica media, thicker tunica externa, presence of valves (shown as pocket-like structures inside the lumen).

**Data source:** Histological descriptions are standard (Gray's Anatomy). Show smooth muscle cell orientation (circular in tunica media, longitudinal at margins). Elastic fiber orientation (wavy, concentric, most prominent in arteries near heart).

**Caption:**
> *The three-layer structure of arteries and veins reflects their different functions. Arteries, exposed to high pressure, have thick muscular walls. Veins, low-pressure vessels, have thin walls and large irregular lumens. The wall thickness ratio (media to external layer) is reversed between the two vessel types. Internal and external elastic membranes appear in large arteries but not in veins.*

## Figure 2: Vessel Types Along the Arterial Pathway

**What to show:** Six panels in a linear sequence from left to right, showing:

1. **Elastic Artery (near heart):** Large lumen (diameter ~10 mm). Thick wall. Concentric wavy lines in the media indicating abundant elastic fibers. Lumen shape: rounded.

2. **Muscular Artery:** Medium lumen (diameter ~2 mm). Thick wall. Many smooth muscle cell bundles in the media (shown as circular cross-sections). Lumen shape: rounded.

3. **Arteriole:** Small lumen (diameter ~50 micrometers). Thin wall. Few smooth muscle cells (1–2 layers). Lumen shape: rounded.

4. **Capillary:** Tiny lumen (diameter ~7 micrometers). One-cell-thick wall (endothelium + basement membrane only). No smooth muscle. Lumen shape: round, barely wide enough for an RBC.

5. **Venule:** Small lumen (diameter ~20 micrometers). Thin wall. Few smooth muscle cells. Slightly irregular lumen.

6. **Vein:** Large lumen (diameter ~5 mm or larger). Thin wall. Thin tunica media. Thick tunica externa. Irregular lumen shape (often collapsed or flattened).

Below each panel, show a diameter scale and note the pressure at that point.

**Data source:** Standard histology. Pressure values from cardiovascular physiology texts.

**Caption:**
> *As blood moves away from the heart, vessels decrease in diameter and wall thickness. Elastic fibers (wavy lines) are most abundant in large arteries. Smooth muscle (circular cross-sections) dominates arterioles, the resistance vessels. Capillaries are one cell thick, optimized for exchange. Veins are largest but thinnest-walled, adapted for low-pressure return.*

## Figure 3: Pressure Drop Across the Vascular Tree

**What to show:** A graph with vessel type on the x-axis and pressure (mm Hg) on the y-axis.

- **X-axis:** Aorta, elastic arteries, muscular arteries, arterioles, capillaries (arterial end and venous end), venules, small veins, large veins, vena cava.

- **Y-axis:** 0 to 140 mm Hg, with gridlines every 20 mm Hg.

- **Data line:** A stepped curve showing pressure at each point. Start at 120 mm Hg (aorta), step down gradually through arteries (115, 110, 100 mm Hg), then steep drop at arterioles (60 mm Hg), gentler drop through capillaries (35 to 18 mm Hg), then gradual drop through veins (15, 10, 5 mm Hg) to near 0 in the vena cava.

- **Resistance visualization:** Add a vertical bar below each segment showing relative resistance. Arterioles have the tallest bar (highest resistance). Capillaries have a moderate bar despite small diameter because they are in parallel. Arteries and veins have short bars.

**Data source:** Standard hemodynamics (Guyton & Hall).

**Caption:**
> *Mean arterial pressure falls continuously from the aorta (~93 mm Hg) to the vena cava (~5 mm Hg). The steepest drop occurs across the arterioles, which account for the majority of systemic vascular resistance. This is why arteriolar diameter is the primary control point for blood pressure regulation.*

## Figure 4: Poiseuille's Law — Effect of Radius on Resistance

**What to show:** A graph showing the relationship between radius and resistance.

- **X-axis:** Vessel radius (as a fraction of normal, from 0.4 to 1.4, or as percent of baseline from 40% to 140%).

- **Y-axis:** Resistance (as a multiple of baseline, from 0 to 10×).

- **Data curve:** A fourth-power curve. When radius is 0.5× (half normal), resistance is 16×. When radius is 0.8× (80% of normal), resistance is 2.44×. When radius is 1.2× (120% of normal), resistance is 0.48×. The curve is steep, showing how small radius changes cause large resistance changes.

- **Annotation boxes:** Label specific points. "Constriction to 50% radius: 16× resistance," "Dilation to 120% radius: 0.48× resistance (52% reduction)."

**Data source:** Poiseuille equation: $R \propto 1/r^4$.

**Caption:**
> *Resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the vessel radius. A 20 percent reduction in radius more than doubles resistance. A 20 percent increase in radius halves resistance. This nonlinear relationship explains why arteriolar diameter changes are such a powerful mechanism for blood pressure control.*

## Figure 5: Capillary Exchange — The Starling Forces

**What to show:** Two adjacent panels.

- **Panel A (left):** A side-view schematic of a capillary bed showing the arterial end, middle, and venous end.

At the **arterial end:** Show an artery feeding into the capillary. Draw the capillary as a hollow tube with a thin wall. Inside, show the lumen (red for blood). Outside, show tissue space (blue for interstitial fluid). Label:
  - CHP = 35 mm Hg (arrow pointing out of capillary).
  - BCOP = 25 mm Hg (arrow pointing into capillary).
  - NFP = 10 mm Hg net outward. Label the direction: filtration (large outward arrow).

At the **middle:** Label:
  - CHP = 25 mm Hg.
  - BCOP = 25 mm Hg.
  - NFP = 0. No net movement.

At the **venous end:** Show the capillary joining a venule. Label:
  - CHP = 18 mm Hg.
  - BCOP = 25 mm Hg.
  - NFP = −7 mm Hg. Label the direction: reabsorption (arrow pointing inward).

Add small dots outside the capillary near the arterial end to represent water molecules moving out. Add dots moving inward near the venous end.

- **Panel B (right):** A graph with position along the capillary on the x-axis (arterial end, middle, venous end) and pressure (mm Hg) on the y-axis (0 to 40).

Show two lines:
  - **Blue line:** CHP declining from 35 to 18 mm Hg.
  - **Red line:** BCOP constant at 25 mm Hg.

Show the area between the lines as shaded green where CHP > BCOP (filtration region) and shaded purple where BCOP > CHP (reabsorption region).

**Data source:** Classic Starling physiology texts.

**Caption:**
> *At the arterial end of a capillary, capillary hydrostatic pressure (blue, 35 mm Hg) exceeds blood colloidal osmotic pressure (red, 25 mm Hg), driving filtration out. As blood travels through the capillary, CHP falls while BCOP remains constant. At the venous end, BCOP exceeds CHP, driving reabsorption in. Approximately 24 liters per day filter out; 20.4 liters are reabsorbed; 3.6 liters are picked up by lymphatic capillaries.*

## Figure 6: Three Types of Capillaries

**What to show:** Three side-by-side panels showing cross-sectional view of each capillary type at high magnification.

- **Panel A (Continuous Capillary):** Endothelium forms a complete lining. Cells are joined by tight junctions (shown as dark lines between cells). Intercellular clefts (gaps) are visible between some cells. Tight junctions are incomplete; they allow water and small molecules through clefts. Show a small water molecule passing through a cleft. Basement membrane intact.

- **Panel B (Fenestrated Capillary):** Endothelium has pores (fenestrations) visible as circular openings in the cells. Tight junctions present but with fenestrations. A glucose molecule is shown passing through a fenestration. Basement membrane intact.

- **Panel C (Sinusoid Capillary):** Endothelium has large intercellular gaps, extensive fenestrations, and an incomplete basement membrane (shown with gaps). Look like Swiss cheese. Blood cells or large plasma proteins are shown crossing the gaps and fenestrations.

Below each panel, list the location (continuous: brain, muscle, lung; fenestrated: kidney, small intestine, glands; sinusoid: liver, spleen, bone marrow) and the primary function.

**Data source:** Standard histology and ultrastructure descriptions from physiology texts.

**Caption:**
> *Capillary structure varies to match tissue needs. Continuous capillaries, with tight junctions and small clefts, allow selective exchange in most tissues. Fenestrated capillaries, with pores in endothelial cells, permit rapid exchange in organs requiring high filtration (kidneys) or absorption (small intestine). Sinusoid capillaries, with large gaps and incomplete basement membrane, enable massive exchange of proteins and cells in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow.*

Use different colors for each segment. Add callout labels noting that veins hold 64 percent of the blood at any time, making them blood reservoirs. During hemorrhage or dehydration, sympathetic activation causes venoconstriction, releasing some of this reserve volume back into the arterial system.

**Caption:**

The pulmonary circulation and heart contain only 16 percent combined, while arteries hold only 10 percent despite their high pressure.*

## Figure 8: The Skeletal Muscle Pump and Respiratory Pump

**What to show:** Two panels.

- **Panel A (Skeletal Muscle Pump):** Show a leg muscle in cross-section with a vein running through it. Draw two states:
  1. **Relaxed muscle:** Muscle fibers are slack. The vein is filled with blood. A valve is shown as a flap inside the vein, pointing upward.
  2. **Contracted muscle:** Muscle fibers are contracted (shown as shortened, bunched). The vein is compressed. Blood is forced upward through the open valve. The valve below the contracting muscle closes, preventing backflow.

Below the diagram, show a time graph of venous pressure in the leg during a contraction-relaxation cycle. Pressure spikes during contraction, drops during relaxation.

- **Panel B (Respiratory Pump):** Show a side view of the thorax. Draw two states:
  1. **Inhalation:** The diaphragm contracts and moves downward (arrow pointing down). The rib cage expands (arrows pointing outward). Thoracic volume increases. Pressure in the thorax decreases. Venous pressure in thoracic veins drops below abdominal venous pressure. Blood flows from abdominal veins into the thorax.
  2. **Exhalation:** The diaphragm relaxes and moves upward. Rib cage narrows. Thoracic volume decreases. Pressure in the thorax increases. Blood is pushed from thoracic veins toward the heart. Valves in the veins prevent backflow.

Add arrows showing blood flow direction in each phase.

**Data source:** Standard physiology of venous return (Guyton & Hall).

**Caption:**
> *The skeletal muscle pump compresses veins during contraction, pushing blood upward against gravity. One-way valves prevent backflow. The respiratory pump uses pressure changes during breathing to draw blood into the thorax during inhalation and push it toward the heart during exhalation. Together, these pumps account for most venous return when the heart rate is low.*

## Figure 9: Clinical Scenario — Edema from Low Plasma Protein

**What to show:** Two capillary diagrams side by side, comparing normal vs. low protein state.

- **Left panel (Normal):** Capillary with:
  - CHP arterial end = 35 mm Hg.
  - BCOP = 25 mm Hg.
  - NFP = 10 mm Hg (filtration at arterial end).
  - CHP venous end = 18 mm Hg.
  - BCOP = 25 mm Hg.
  - NFP = −7 mm Hg (reabsorption at venous end).
  - Tissue fluid (blue) around capillary is shown as normal level (not swollen).

- **Right panel (Low Protein/Malnutrition):** Same capillary, but:
  - CHP arterial end = 35 mm Hg (unchanged).
  - BCOP = 15 mm Hg (reduced due to low plasma protein).
  - NFP = 20 mm Hg (filtration increased).
  - CHP venous end = 18 mm Hg.
  - BCOP = 15 mm Hg.
  - NFP = 3 mm Hg (reabsorption reduced).
  - Tissue fluid (blue) is shown as swollen, with fluid accumulating around the capillary and the tissue space appearing enlarged.

Add annotations: "More fluid filtered out" at arterial end, "Less fluid reabsorbed" at venous end, "Net fluid accumulation → edema" in tissue.

**Data source:** Clinical pathophysiology (edema in malnutrition, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome).

**Caption:**
> *In malnutrition or liver disease, plasma protein levels fall, reducing blood colloidal osmotic pressure. This reduces the osmotic pull that draws fluid back into capillaries. More fluid filters out at the arterial end; less is reabsorbed at the venous end. The imbalance leads to fluid accumulation in tissues (edema).*
