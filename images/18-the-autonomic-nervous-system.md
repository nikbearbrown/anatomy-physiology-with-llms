## [FIGURE 1: Sympathetic vs. Parasympathetic Anatomy]

**Purpose:** Show the structural basis of divergence (sympathetic) vs. precision (parasympathetic).

**Content:**
- Left side: Sympathetic pathway. Spinal cord (thoracolumbar region highlighted). Preganglionic fiber emerging, short, to sympathetic chain ganglion adjacent to vertebral column. One preganglionic neuron branching to multiple postganglionic neurons. Long postganglionic fibers radiating to distant organs (heart, lungs, blood vessels, sweat glands, iris).
- Right side: Parasympathetic pathway. Brainstem with CN III, CN VII, CN IX, CN X emerging. Long preganglionic fiber traveling to ganglion near or within target organ. One preganglionic neuron synapsing on one or a few postganglionic neurons. Short postganglionic fiber within organ wall.
- Labels: preganglionic (myelinated, fast), postganglionic (unmyelinated, slow), chain ganglion, terminal ganglion, target organs.

**Key visual principle:** The page layout makes the structural difference immediately obvious—sympathetic spreads wide; parasympathetic is tight and local.

## [FIGURE 2: Neurotransmitter Map — Two Synapses, Three Molecules]

**Purpose:** Clarify that the same neurotransmitter at the preganglionic synapse leads to different molecules at the postganglionic synapse.

**Content:**
Three columns: **Preganglionic Synapse** | **Postganglionic Synapse (Parasympathetic)** | **Postganglionic Synapse (Sympathetic)**

- Row 1: Neurotransmitter released
  - All preganglionic: Acetylcholine (ACh)
  - Parasympathetic postganglionic: Acetylcholine (ACh)
  - Sympathetic postganglionic: Norepinephrine (NE) [with note: "except sweat glands and skeletal muscle vessels"]

- Row 2: Receptor type on target
  - All preganglionic target: Nicotinic (ligand-gated cation channel)
  - Parasympathetic postganglionic target: Muscarinic (G-protein-coupled)
  - Sympathetic postganglionic target: Adrenergic α or β (G-protein-coupled)

- Row 3: Effect on target
  - Preganglionic: Fast depolarization (always excitatory)
  - Parasympathetic: Varied (can inhibit, can excite, depending on muscarinic subtype and organ)
  - Sympathetic: Varied (can excite via β, can constrict via α)

**Visual principle:** Use color-coding (one color for ACh, another for NE) to make the distinction clear. The key insight is that the *same molecule* (ACh) can lead to opposite effects depending on the *receptor type*.

## [FIGURE 3: Baroreceptor Reflex Loop — A Visceral Reflex]

**Purpose:** Show how sensory input, integration, and motor output work together in a concrete autonomic reflex.

**Content:**
Circular diagram with five stages:

1. **Sensory Input:** Carotid artery wall. Baroreceptor (stretch receptor) embedded in wall. Arrow labeled "Stretch" → "Increased firing rate."

2. **Afferent (Sensory) Pathway:** Glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) carrying sensory signals from baroreceptor, entering the medulla.

3. **Integration Center:** Solitary nucleus in medulla (shown as a circle with many incoming and outgoing connections). Input from baroreceptor. Integrates with other signals (from hypothalamus, from other brainstem nuclei).

4. **Efferent (Motor) Pathway:** Two branches:
   - Parasympathetic: Vagus nerve (CN X) preganglionic fibers → ganglia in heart → postganglionic fibers release ACh → muscarinic receptors on sinoatrial node.
   - Sympathetic: Inhibition symbol (⊣) on thoracic spinal cord preganglionic neurons (fewer fibers fire).

5. **Effect on Target:** Sinoatrial node (pacemaker). When parasympathetic strong + sympathetic weak → slower depolarization → lower heart rate. When parasympathetic weak + sympathetic strong → faster depolarization → higher heart rate.

**Feedback loop:** Heart rate change → change in blood pressure → change in baroreceptor firing → cycle continues.

**Visual principle:** The circular layout emphasizes that this is a continuous feedback loop, not a one-time event. Color-code (green for parasympathetic, red for sympathetic) to distinguish the competing pathways.

## [FIGURE 4: Hypothalamus-Medulla-Heart Pathway — Central Control of Cardiovascular Autonomic Tone]

**Purpose:** Show how emotional/cognitive information flows from the brain to the heart via autonomic circuits.

**Content:**
Three levels:

**Level 1 (Forebrain):**
- Amygdala (shown in temporal lobe, labeled as emotion/fear center).
- Hypothalamus (at base of brain, above pituitary).
- Arrow from amygdala → hypothalamus, labeled "medial forebrain bundle."
- Label: "Emotional input: fear, anxiety."

**Level 2 (Brainstem):**
- Medulla shown with cardiovascular center highlighted.
- Two major output nuclei: nucleus ambiguus and dorsal motor nucleus (parasympathetic) on left; sympathetic preganglionic neurons in upper thoracic spinal cord on right.
- Arrows labeled: dorsal longitudinal fasciculus (hypothalamus → brainstem) and direct spinal connections.

**Level 3 (Heart):**
- Sinoatrial node (pacemaker).
- Left side: postganglionic parasympathetic fiber releasing ACh onto muscarinic receptor. Effect: slower depolarization.
- Right side: postganglionic sympathetic fiber releasing NE onto beta-adrenergic receptor. Effect: faster depolarization.
- Label: "Competing signals at target organ. Net effect determines heart rate."

**Visual principle:** Use vertical layout to show hierarchical flow: emotion at top → brain centers in middle → heart at bottom. Color-code emotional (e.g., red warning) → brain (blue/purple) → cardiac (green for parasympathetic, red for sympathetic).

## [FIGURE 5: The Pupil — Competing Inputs in Real Time]

**Purpose:** Concrete example of antagonistic innervation and how the stronger signal wins.

**Content:**
Two columns: **Low Light** and **Bright Light**

**Low Light column:**
- Retina with low photon input.
- Optic nerve signal to hypothalamus.
- Hypothalamus activates sympathetic: upper thoracic spinal cord → superior cervical ganglion → postganglionic fiber to iris.
- Postganglionic fiber releases norepinephrine.
- Radial fibers of iris (smooth muscle) contract.
- Pupil dilates (wider opening for more light).

**Bright Light column:**
- Retina with high photon input.
- Optic nerve signal to hypothalamus.
- Hypothalamus activates parasympathetic: oculomotor nerve (CN III) → ciliary ganglion → postganglionic fiber to iris.
- Postganglionic fiber releases acetylcholine.
- Circular fibers of iris (smooth muscle) contract.
- Pupil constricts (narrower opening for less light).

**Side-by-side comparison:** Shows how the *same organ* (iris) receives opposite commands and responds to the stronger one.

**Visual principle:** Use arrows pointing in opposite directions (outward for radial, inward for circular) to emphasize the mechanical antagonism. Show pupil size changing between the two panels.

## [FIGURE 6: Why Nicotine Causes Arrhythmias — Unequal Parasympathetic Coverage]

**Purpose:** Explain why nicotine's activating effect on both autonomic ganglia leads to cardiovascular problems.

**Content:**
Two panels: **Most Organs (e.g., Digestive Tract)** and **Heart & Blood Vessels**

**Panel 1: Most Organs**
- Organ receives postganglionic sympathetic fiber (releases NE → adrenergic effect: activate) AND postganglionic parasympathetic fiber (releases ACh → muscarinic effect: inhibit).
- When nicotine activates both ganglia equally, both postganglionic fibers fire at the same time.
- Competing signals cancel or balance.
- Result: No net change in organ function.

**Panel 2: Heart & Blood Vessels**
- Blood vessels: Receive postganglionic sympathetic fibers (NE → constriction) but almost NO parasympathetic input.
- When nicotine activates both ganglia equally, sympathetic postganglionic fibers fire strongly, but parasympathetic postganglionic fibers have no target. Nicotin increases sympathetic tone on vessels.
- Result: Blood pressure rises.

- Heart (sinoatrial node): Receives postganglionic sympathetic fibers (NE → faster depolarization, higher rate) AND postganglionic parasympathetic fibers (ACh → slower depolarization, lower rate).
- When nicotine activates both ganglia equally, the heart receives conflicting signals simultaneously. Nicotine does not resolve the conflict because the mechanism of sympathetic speeding is different from the mechanism of parasympathetic slowing.
- Result: Irregular rhythms (arrhythmias).

**Visual principle:** Use the same organ symbols (blood vessel, heart) but show different distributions of postganglionic innervation. Use arrows to show the unequal coverage: vessels get only sympathetic, heart gets both.

## [FIGURE 7: Autonomic Tone — Why Different Organs Have Different Defaults]

**Purpose:** Illustrate the concept that the resting state of an organ depends on which division has more baseline activity.

**Content:**
Two rows: **Heart (Parasympathetic Tone)** and **Blood Vessels (Sympathetic Tone)**

**Heart row:**
- Intrinsic rate (no autonomic input): ~100 bpm.
- With baseline parasympathetic activity: ~70 bpm (vagus nerve constantly slightly active).
- Resting tone = **parasympathetic**. The organ's "default" is held back by the parasympathetic system.
- During sympathetic activation (exercise): 120–140 bpm.

**Blood Vessels row:**
- With no input: Vessels would be neither maximally constricted nor dilated.
- With baseline sympathetic activity: Slight constriction (baseline blood pressure).
- Resting tone = **sympathetic**. The organ's "default" is set by sympathetic input.
- Why? Because parasympathetic fibers barely innervate systemic blood vessels. The sympathetic system has the floor.
- During parasympathetic activation (localized, e.g., in reproductive organs): Vasodilation (erection).

**Visual principle:** Use a "default position" metaphor (like a door that is usually held open by one team and closed by the other). Show the resting position vs. the activated position.

## Additional Notes for Illustrator

- **Color scheme:** Green for parasympathetic (rest, restore, digest), red for sympathetic (alert, mobilize, act), blue for sensory/afferent, orange for central (brain), black for structural boundaries.
- **Anatomical accuracy:** All fiber tracts, nuclei, and organs should match human anatomy as depicted in standard anatomy texts (Gray's, Moore's).
- **Notation:** Use standard symbols for synapses (filled circles for transmitter release, open circles for receptors), axons (lines), cell bodies (circles or ellipses).
- **Labeling:** Include both common names (vagus nerve) and anatomical designations (CN X).
- **Scale:** Some figures (e.g., Figure 3, baroreceptor) are cellular; others (e.g., Figure 4, hypothalamus-heart) are systems-level. Make scale differences clear.
