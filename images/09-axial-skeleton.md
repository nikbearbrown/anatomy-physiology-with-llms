# Images: 09-axial-skeleton

## Image 1: Skull, Anterior View

**Placement:** Chapter opening, after first cold-open paragraph about the skull being 22 pieces of bone

**Description:** Front-facing view of human skull showing both the braincase and facial skeleton. The braincase should be rendered in a slightly different tone (or with suture lines emphasized) to distinguish it from the facial bones beneath. Major foramina should be labeled: orbits (eye sockets), nasal cavity aperture, foramen magnum visible from front in this view or noted as being posterior.

**Pedagogical purpose:** To show the reader what a 22-piece structure looks like as a unit. To emphasize that the skull is not smooth — there are lines where bones meet. To provide reference for the "two designs stacked" concept.

**Style notes:** Clean, clear labeling. No artistic rendering — anatomically accurate. Color coding optional: consider teal for braincase, beige for facial bones to emphasize the functional distinction.

## Image 2: Cervical Vertebrae (C1 and C2) — Functional Relationship

**Placement:** Concept 2 section, after the atlas/axis explanation

**Description:** Show C1 (atlas) from superior view, indicating the flat articular surfaces that receive the skull's occipital condyles. Show C2 (axis) from anterior and lateral views, with the dens prominently displayed. Also show C1 and C2 articulated together, lateral view, to show how the atlas's anterior arch wraps around the dens.

**Pedagogical purpose:** To make clear why these two bones are different and what they do. To show the mechanical relationship — the dens as a pivot pin, the anterior arch as a collar.

**Style notes:** Use arrows or motion indicators to show the nod (C1 on skull) and the turn (C1 on C2). Multiple views are essential here because the relationship cannot be understood from a single angle.

---

## Image 3: Typical Vertebra — Full Anatomy

**Placement:** Concept 2, after "The repeating unit" section

**Description:** Lateral (side) and posterior (back) views of a typical vertebra (from midthoracic region, e.g., T6). Label all seven processes, the body, the vertebral arch, the pedicles, the laminae, the vertebral foramen, and the intervertebral foramina (shown in relation to adjacent vertebra). Show the size relationship to a quarter or other reference object to give scale.

**Pedagogical purpose:** To give the reader a clear map of the anatomy. To show that a vertebra is not simple — it has many components, each with a function.

**Style notes:** Posterior view should show how the spinous process points backward, where muscles attach. Lateral view should show how the body faces forward (for weight-bearing) and the arch behind it (for spinal cord protection).

---

## Image 4: Vertebral Column — Full Length, Showing Regional Variation

**Placement:** Concept 2, early in the "Mechanism" section

**Description:** Full lateral view of adult spine, showing C1-C7, T1-T12, L1-L5, sacrum (fused), coccyx (fused). Use different colors or shading to show the four curvatures: cervical (inward), thoracic (outward), lumbar (inward), sacrococcygeal (outward). Include a side-by-side comparison showing fetal curvature (one long outward curve) and adult (four separate curves).

**Pedagogical purpose:** To show the complexity of the spine as a whole. To illustrate the developmental transition from one curve to four curves. To provide reference for the regional specializations discussed in the chapter.

**Style notes:** Use color or subtle shading to distinguish regions. Include annotations showing what each region does (cervical = mobile, thoracic = stabilized by ribs, lumbar = weight-bearing). Show the size progression: cervical vertebrae small, thoracic medium, lumbar large.

---

## Image 5: Intervertebral Disc — Cross-Section and Herniation

**Placement:** Concept 2, in the disc explanation section

**Description:** Superior (top-down) view of a healthy disc, showing the anulus fibrosus (outer ring of fibers, radiating like a wheel) and the nucleus pulposus (gel-like center). Also show a lateral view of two vertebrae with a disc between them, labeled. Then show a second disc with herniation — the nucleus bulging posteriorly (toward the spinal canal) and slightly to one side, with the nerve exiting through the intervertebral foramen shown being compressed.

**Pedagogical purpose:** To explain the structure and function of the disc. To show why herniation is possible and why it causes pain (nerve compression).

**Style notes:** Use color to distinguish the fibrous layer (darker, more organized) from the gel core (lighter, more fluid-looking). The herniation image should clearly show the nucleus pushing against the nerve path.

---

## Image 6: Thoracic Cage — Anterior and Lateral Views

**Placement:** Concept 3 section opening

**Description:** Anterior view showing the sternum (manubrium, body, xiphoid process), all 12 pairs of ribs, and the costal cartilages connecting them to the sternum. Color-code or label to distinguish true ribs (1-7, direct attachment), false ribs (8-10, indirect attachment via rib 7), and floating ribs (11-12, no sternal attachment). Lateral view showing a single rib's full length from vertebral attachment to costal cartilage.

**Pedagogical purpose:** To show the cage as a complete structure. To illustrate the different rib types and their attachments. To provide scale.

**Style notes:** In anterior view, show how the ribs curve around the body in a barrel shape. In lateral view, show the rib articulating at two points (body and transverse process) on the thoracic vertebra, and the curved path to the front.

---

## Image 7: Rib Articulation with Thoracic Vertebra — Detail

**Placement:** Concept 3, after rib explanation

**Description:** Superior (top-down) view of a thoracic vertebra showing the two costal facets (one on the body, one on the transverse process) and how a rib articulates at both points. Lateral view showing the rib head (articulates with body and body of next vertebra above), rib neck, rib tubercle (articulates with transverse process), rib angle (greatest curvature), costal groove on undersurface, and the rib body extending anteriorly.

**Pedagogical purpose:** To explain the dual-articulation system that permits the rocking motion necessary for breathing.

**Style notes:** Use arrows to show the axes of motion. Indicate which articulation permits rocking (at the body) and which permits pivoting (at the transverse process).

---

## Image 8: Spinal Curvatures — Development and Function

**Placement:** Concept 2, in the curvature explanation section

**Description:** Side-by-side images: (1) Infant spine showing the single fetal curvature (one long outward curve); (2) Adult spine showing the four curvatures; (3) Close-up of an intervertebral disc under compression (showing how the discs compress anteriorly when bent, absorbing shock). Include annotations explaining primary curves (thoracic, sacrococcygeal — present from fetal development) vs. secondary curves (cervical, lumbar — develop after birth).

**Pedagogical purpose:** To show the developmental story. To explain why the curves are there — they increase shock absorption and weight-bearing capacity.

**Style notes:** Use exaggeration slightly in showing the disc compression — it makes the mechanism clearer. Consider showing a vertical load arrow to indicate weight distribution.

---

## Image 9: Skull Sutures and Fontanelles (Infant)

**Placement:** Concept 1, in the trade-off section

**Description:** Superior (top-down) view of infant skull showing the major fontanelles: anterior (larger diamond-shaped fontanelle at front, between frontal and parietal bones), posterior (smaller triangular fontanelle at back, between parietal and occipital), and the major suture lines connecting them (sagittal suture running front to back, coronal suture running side to side, lambdoid suture at back).

**Pedagogical purpose:** To show where growth happens. To make clear that the infant skull is not fully fused and to explain why this matters for birth and growth.

**Style notes:** Render the fontanelles in a lighter color or with a distinct pattern to distinguish them from bone. Label the sutures and fontanelles clearly. Consider a separate image showing how the skull bones compress slightly as the head passes through the birth canal.

---

## Image 10: Load Path Through the Axial Skeleton

**Placement:** Integration section

**Description:** Lateral view of the full skeleton from skull to feet, with arrows showing the path of load/weight from the skull → cervical vertebrae → thoracic vertebrae → lumbar vertebrae → sacrum → pelvis → femurs → tibia/fibula → feet. Each segment should be labeled with its weight-bearing function.

**Pedagogical purpose:** To show how the axial skeleton is integrated into the whole-body load path. To illustrate that weight from the head is transferred all the way to the ground.

**Style notes:** Use color or arrow thickness to indicate increasing load (head is lighter load; lumbar carries total body weight). Show the mechanics of how each region transfers load to the next.

---

## Commissioning Notes

All images should be:
- Anatomically accurate (reference current Gray's Anatomy or OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology)
- Clear and uncluttered (no unnecessary detail that distracts from the teaching point)
- Labeled with terms defined in the chapter (no unexplained anatomical jargon in the images)
- Showing scale (where relevant, include a reference object)
- Suitable for black-and-white reproduction (color is nice but not required)

Images should NOT:
- Show artistic flourishes that add no teaching value
- Use colors that are difficult to distinguish for colorblind readers (avoid red-green combinations without additional distinction)
- Include extra anatomical structures that are not referenced in the chapter text
