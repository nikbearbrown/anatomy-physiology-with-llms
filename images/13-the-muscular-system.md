## Chapter-Level Visuals (Embed or Note for Art Phase)

### [FIGURE: Three Lever Classes in the Body]
**Purpose**: Show why third-class levers dominate the skeletal system.

**Components**:
- **First-class lever**: Fulcrum in center (like seesaw). Example: skull on C1 vertebra. Show skull, vertebra, neck muscles pulling back, face weight pulling forward. Label: "Balanced—neither force nor speed advantage."
- **Second-class lever**: Load in middle (like wheelbarrow). Example: calf muscle, heel lift. Show foot, ball of foot as fulcrum, calf muscle pulling at heel (effort), body weight on toes (load). Label: "Mechanical advantage—rare in body."
- **Third-class lever**: Effort in middle (like tweezers). Example: biceps flexion. Show forearm, elbow as fulcrum, biceps pulling close to elbow (effort), hand at distance (load). Label: "Speed and range—dominant in limbs."

**Caption**: "The body uses mostly third-class levers, trading strength for speed and range of motion. A muscle that attaches close to the joint (effort close to fulcrum) moves the far end of the limb through a large arc with minimal muscle contraction distance."

### [FIGURE: Five Fascicle Arrangements & Their Force-Range Trade-Off]
**Purpose**: Show how internal muscle organization determines force vs. range.

**Components** (five panels, each showing cross-section and side view):

1. **Parallel (fusiform)**: Fibers run straight along muscle axis. Bulges in middle (belly). Example: biceps brachii.
   - Label: "Long contraction distance, moderate force, good range."

2. **Circular (Sphincter)**: Fibers arrange concentrically around an opening. Example: orbicularis oris.
   - Label: "Specialized for controlling access. Contraction size varies with opening diameter."

3. **Convergent**: Fibers spread from wide origin, converge to point. Example: pectoralis major.
   - Label: "Flexible: can pull from multiple angles. Moderate force, moderate range."

4. **Unipennate**: Fascicles attach to one side of central tendon at an angle. Example: extensor digitorum.
   - Label: "More fibers packed per volume. Higher force, shorter contraction range."

5. **Bipennate**: Fascicles attach to both sides of tendon at an angle. Example: rectus femoris.
   - Label: "Maximum fiber packing. Highest force per volume. Limited range."

**Comparison grid at bottom**:
| Arrangement | Force | Range | Use Case |
|-------------|-------|-------|----------|
| Parallel | Moderate | Good | General movement |
| Pennate | High | Low | Power movements |

**Caption**: "Muscle fibers organize according to the task. A parallel muscle 10cm long contracts through its full length. A pennate muscle of the same size doesn't—its fibers are angled, so they fit more fibers per volume but contract less distance. The body optimized for the job: pennate for power (leg extension, jumping), parallel for range (arm reaching, spinning)."

### [FIGURE: Forearm Flexion—Three Muscles, Three Attachment Positions]
**Purpose**: Show how the same action (forearm flexion) uses different muscles based on attachment position and load.

**Three panels showing biceps, brachialis, brachioradialis**:

1. **Biceps brachii**:
   - Origin: Scapula (shoulder blade)
   - Insertion: Radius (thumb-side forearm bone), close to elbow
   - Arrow showing short contraction pulling forearm up
   - Label: "Fast, light-load flexion. Inserts CLOSE to fulcrum = long-range movement."

2. **Brachialis** (beneath biceps):
   - Origin: Humerus (upper arm bone)
   - Insertion: Ulna (pinky-side forearm bone), even closer to elbow than biceps
   - Arrow showing contraction
   - Label: "Heavy-load assist. Most medial insertion = extra force when needed."

3. **Brachioradialis**:
   - Origin: Near elbow on humerus
   - Insertion: Far down radius (near wrist)
   - Arrow showing contraction
   - Label: "Heavy or gravity-loaded flexion. Inserts FAR from fulcrum = greater force, less speed."

**Comparison inset**:
"Light cup → Biceps fires. Heavy bag → Brachialis joins. Fighting gravity → Brachioradialis dominates. Same joint. Same action. Different muscles. Different attachment positions."

**Caption**: "A muscle's attachment point determines its mechanical advantage. The biceps inserts close to the elbow (the fulcrum), so it moves fast but with less relative force. The brachioradialis inserts far from the elbow, so it pulls harder but moves less distance. The nervous system chooses which muscle to emphasize based on load and speed needs."

### [FIGURE: Turning Your Head—Muscle Coordination Roles]
**Purpose**: Show how one intended movement (head rotation) recruits muscles in four roles (agonist, antagonist, synergist, fixator).

**Top view of head, showing left turn (rotate head to left)**:

**Right side of neck** (active side):
- Sternocleidomastoid (large, diagonal): Labeled "AGONIST—does the rotation"
- Semispinalis capitis (back of neck): Labeled "SYNERGIST—assists, adds power"
- Small rotator muscles near C1-C2: Labeled "STABILIZERS—control fine motion"

**Left side of neck** (opposing side):
- Sternocleidomastoid: Labeled "ANTAGONIST—suppressed, controls return speed"

**Shoulder region**:
- Trapezius (both sides, lightly shaded): Labeled "FIXATORS—stabilize shoulder girdle as anchor point"

**Cervical spine**:
- Erector spinae and deep stabilizers: Labeled "STABILIZERS—prevent unwanted spinal side-to-side motion"

**Inset arrow showing reciprocal inhibition**: 
"When right SCM fires → nervous system suppresses left SCM. No fighting. Smooth motion."

**Caption**: "Turning your head is not one muscle. It's an orchestra. The sternocleidomastoid on one side pulls the head; its partner on the other side is actively suppressed by the nervous system (reciprocal inhibition). Muscles behind the neck assist (synergists). Shoulder muscles stabilize. Spine muscles brace. All coordinated without conscious thought. This is muscle coordination."

## Visual Style Notes

**Attenborough × Feynman aesthetic**:
- Clean, scientific. No cartoon style.
- Color usage: Anatomically accurate (muscle in red/pink), bone in pale gray, tendons in tan, nerve signal in blue for clarity.
- Labels are specific: "Rectus femoris" not just "thigh muscle."
- Arrows show force direction and contraction. Motion is clear.
- Insets show scale (human silhouette or hand size reference where helpful).

**Avoid**:
- Decorative flourishes
- Redundant labeling
- Oversimplified "cute" anatomy
- Cluttered backgrounds

**Prefer**:
- White or very light gray background
- High contrast between anatomical structures
- Scale bars where dimensions matter
- Directional arrows for force and motion
