# Images & Figures — The Tissue Level of Organization

Strategic guide for image placement, sourcing, and pedagogical purpose.

---

## IMAGE 1: Cell Junctions (Three Types)

**Placement:** Concept 1, after "The Mechanism: Sheet and Seal" section, following the explanation of tight, anchoring, and gap junctions.

**Size & Format:** Three-panel figure, landscape orientation. ~15 cm wide on a printed page.

**What it shows:**
- **Panel A (Tight Junction):** Cross-section of two epithelial cells with claudins/occludins proteins forming a seal across the intercellular space. Show the "zipper" pattern. Label: claudins, occludins, sealed pathway, blocked extracellular space.
- **Panel B (Anchoring Junction / Desmosome):** Cross-section of two epithelial cells with cadherins extending across the intercellular space, attached to intracellular plaques (patches of structural protein). Label: cadherin, desmosomal plaque, actin filaments, intercellular space.
- **Panel C (Gap Junction):** Cross-section showing connexin proteins forming a channel (connexon) between two cells. Show small molecules (glucose, ions) passing through. Label: connexin, connexon, gap, passageway.

**Pedagogical purpose:** 
Students have just read that there are three types of junctions. The figure makes the structural difference visible — sealed vs. gripped vs. connected. This is the first time they see that molecular structures determine tissue properties.

**Caption:**
"Cell junctions in epithelial tissue. Tight junctions (A) seal the space between cells, creating a selective barrier. Anchoring junctions (B) mechanically grip adjacent cells, preventing them from separating under stress. Gap junctions (C) form molecular channels, allowing chemical communication between cells. All three types are usually present in a single epithelial tissue."

**Source:** Modified from OpenStax (figure-ch04_02_01 or equivalent). Ensure clarity on the molecular scale (proteins are the key visual).

**Accessibility note:** Color-code the three junction types (tight = red, anchoring = blue, gap = green) consistently throughout the chapter if referred to again.

## IMAGE 2: Epithelial Cell Types (Squamous, Cuboidal, Columnar)

**Placement:** Concept 1, in the section discussing epithelial classification ("Classification of Epithelial Tissues" section).

**Size & Format:** Three vertical columns, each showing a cell in profile. ~12 cm wide.

**What it shows:**
- **Column A (Squamous):** Thin, flattened cell. Nucleus barely visible, stretched horizontally. Label: nucleus, basement membrane.
- **Column B (Cuboidal):** Box-like cell, roughly as wide as it is tall. Nucleus in the center. Label: nucleus, basement membrane.
- **Column C (Columnar):** Tall, rectangular cell, taller than it is wide. Nucleus in the basal end. Label: nucleus, cilia (if ciliated variant), basement membrane.

**Pedagogical purpose:**
The student learns that epithelial cell shape is a functional signature. Thin cells indicate rapid diffusion. Cube cells indicate active transport and secretion. Tall cells indicate secretion or absorption. This figure makes that relationship visual.

**Caption:**
"Epithelial cell types are classified by shape. Squamous cells are thin and flat, optimized for rapid diffusion. Cuboidal cells are compact and active in absorption and secretion. Columnar cells are tall and lined with cilia or microvilli, specialized for absorption and transport. The shape of the cell is a clue to its function."

**Source:** OpenStax figure-ch04_02_02 or equivalent. Ensure color-coded consistently (same cell shapes appear again in the stratified epithelium figure).

**Cross-reference note:** When introducing simple vs. stratified epithelium, use the same cell-shape color coding for consistency.

## IMAGE 3: Simple vs. Stratified Epithelium

**Placement:** Concept 1, immediately after the epithelial cell types figure, in the section "Classification of Epithelial Tissues."

**Size & Format:** Two side-by-side columns. ~15 cm wide.

**What it shows:**
- **Left panel (Simple Epithelium):** Single layer of cells (use one of the three shapes: squamous, cuboidal, or columnar). All cells rest on the basement membrane. Label: apical surface, cells, basement membrane, underlying connective tissue.
- **Right panel (Stratified Epithelium):** Multiple stacked layers. Only the basal layer touches the basement membrane. Show squamous cells (flattened) on top, cuboidal cells below. Label: apical surface, stratified layers, basal layer, basement membrane, underlying connective tissue.

**Pedagogical purpose:**
Students need to see that "simple" means one layer, "stratified" means multiple layers. The basal layer is where stem cells are. The apical layer is what touches the outside world. This spatial organization determines regeneration capacity and protection.

**Caption:**
"Simple epithelium (left) consists of a single cell layer resting on the basement membrane. It is optimized for absorption and diffusion. Stratified epithelium (right) consists of multiple stacked layers, with only the basal layer resting on the basement membrane. It is optimized for protection against mechanical and chemical wear. The basal layer contains stem cells that continuously divide to replace surface cells."

**Source:** OpenStax figure-ch04_02_02 (often combined in one image).

**Note:** If the original source image shows all six combinations (3 shapes × 2 layer types), it is fine. But the caption should focus students on the layer difference first.

## IMAGE 4: Connective Tissue Types (Five Varieties)

**Placement:** Concept 2, after the section "The Mechanism: Collagen and Ground" explaining the five types.

**Size & Format:** Five microscopic images arranged in a grid (2 rows, with one spanning the bottom). ~18 cm wide.

**What it shows:**

- **Panel A (Loose Connective Tissue):** Microscopic view showing scattered cells (fibroblasts), widely spaced collagen fibers, and abundant gel-like ground substance. The space dominates. Label: fibroblast, collagen fiber, ground substance, extracellular space.

- **Panel B (Dense Connective Tissue):** Microscopic view showing tightly packed collagen fibers, often aligned in one direction (if parallel alignment) or in multiple directions (if woven). Fewer cells visible. Label: collagen fiber, fibroblast, ground substance (less prominent).

- **Panel C (Cartilage):** Microscopic view showing cells (chondrocytes) in small cavities (lacunae) within a firm matrix. Label: chondrocyte, lacuna, cartilage matrix, no blood vessels.

- **Panel D (Bone):** Microscopic view showing cells (osteocytes) in lacunae within a calcified matrix. Haversian canals (small channels carrying blood vessels) visible. Label: osteocyte, lacuna, calcified matrix, Haversian canal.

- **Panel E (Blood):** Microscopic view showing red blood cells (discs), white blood cells (nucleated), platelets, in a light-colored plasma. Label: red blood cell, white blood cell, platelet, plasma.

**Pedagogical purpose:**
Students see that all five connective tissues follow the same strategy (cells + matrix), but the matrix composition and fiber density differ. This unifies the category while showing diversity. The images make the differences obvious.

**Caption:**
"Connective tissues vary widely in appearance and properties, but all consist of cells embedded in an extracellular matrix of proteins and ground substance. Loose connective tissue (A) has sparse fibers and abundant gel-like ground substance, providing flexibility. Dense connective tissue (B) has tightly packed collagen fibers, providing strength. Cartilage (C) is a firm gel, optimized for bearing load and providing smooth surfaces. Bone (D) is calcified, providing rigidity and load support. Blood (E) is a fluid connective tissue where cells are suspended in plasma, providing transport."

**Source:** OpenStax figures for connective tissues (usually in the section "Classification of Connective Tissues"). Ensure bone shows Haversian systems clearly.

**Accessibility note:** Color-code the cells vs. matrix in all five panels for visual consistency.

## IMAGE 5: Muscle Tissue Types (Skeletal, Cardiac, Smooth)

**Placement:** Concept 3, after the section "The Mechanism: Contraction and Conduction" describing muscle types.

**Size & Format:** Three microscopic images arranged horizontally. ~15 cm wide.

**What it shows:**

- **Panel A (Skeletal Muscle):** Microscopic image showing elongated, striated fibers (visible bands/striations). Multiple nuclei per cell visible at the periphery. Label: striation, nucleus, myofibril.

- **Panel B (Cardiac Muscle):** Microscopic image showing branched, striated fibers with dark zones (intercalated discs) connecting them. Single or double nuclei per cell, centrally located. Label: striation, intercalated disc, nucleus, branching.

- **Panel C (Smooth Muscle):** Microscopic image showing spindle-shaped cells, NO striations visible. Single nucleus per cell, centrally located. Label: nucleus, smooth muscle cell, no striations.

**Pedagogical purpose:**
The striation is the visual signature of the sarcomere. Skeletal and cardiac are striated; smooth is not. The branching and intercalated discs are unique to cardiac. These visual differences map onto functional differences (voluntary vs. automatic, fatigue properties, conduction speed).

**Caption:**
"Muscle tissue types show distinct microscopic features that correlate with function. Skeletal muscle (A) is striated and multinucleated, with nuclei at the cell periphery. It is under voluntary control and fatigues quickly. Cardiac muscle (B) is striated, with branched cells connected via intercalated discs (gap junctions), and centrally located nuclei. It contracts automatically and synchronously. Smooth muscle (C) lacks striations and has spindle-shaped cells with central nuclei. It contracts slowly and can maintain tension with minimal energy."

**Source:** OpenStax muscle tissue figures (usually figure-ch04_04_01 or similar). Ensure magnification is roughly consistent so students can see the relative size differences.

## IMAGE 6: Neuron Structure

**Placement:** Concept 3, after the section introducing nervous tissue and the neuron.

**Size & Format:** Single large diagram, landscape. ~18 cm wide. Show the neuron with roughly accurate proportions (cell body ~20 micrometers, axon can extend a meter).

**What it shows:**
- **Soma (cell body):** Contains nucleus and most organelles. The decision-making center.
- **Dendrites:** Multiple branching extensions receiving input from other neurons. Label: dendrite.
- **Axon:** Single long extension transmitting output to other cells. Label: axon, axon terminal (synaptic knob).
- **Axon terminal:** The output end, where neurotransmitters are released. Label: synaptic vesicles, axon terminal, synaptic cleft (if showing synapse).
- **Proportions:** If showing to scale, note that the axon is often the longest part of the cell (up to a meter in spinal motor neurons, but the soma is only 20 micrometers). A scale bar is essential.

**Pedagogical purpose:**
The neuron's asymmetry is its power. Input on one end, output on the other. The long axon enables long-distance signaling. This figure makes that architecture clear.

**Caption:**
"A neuron consists of a soma (cell body), dendrites (input receptors), and an axon (output transmitter). The soma contains the nucleus and organelles. Dendrites receive signals from other neurons. The axon, sometimes extending a meter or more, transmits the neuron's signal to other cells. At the axon terminal, neurotransmitters are released into the synaptic cleft, transmitting the signal to the next cell. The neuron's architecture enables communication across distance."

**Source:** Custom diagram or OpenStax neuron figure. Ensure clarity on the signal flow direction (dendrite → soma → axon).

**Scale note:** Include a scale bar or note if the proportions are not to scale. If shown to scale, the axon terminal would be microscopic; a magnified inset may be helpful.

## IMAGE 7: The Motor Unit (Optional, for Integration Section)

**Placement:** Concept 3, in the "Case Study: The Motor Unit" section.

**Size & Format:** Diagram showing a single motor neuron with axon branching to multiple muscle fibers. ~15 cm wide.

**What it shows:**
- Motor neuron soma (in spinal cord, background).
- Single axon exiting the cord and branching.
- Axon terminal (neuromuscular junction) at each muscle fiber.
- Multiple muscle fibers (all contracting together when the neuron fires).
- Label: motor neuron, axon branches, muscle fiber, neuromuscular junction.

**Pedagogical purpose:**
Students see that one neuron controls many muscle fibers. All the muscle fibers it controls contract simultaneously. The ratio of fibers per neuron determines the precision of control (small ratio = fine control, large ratio = gross movement).

**Caption:**
"A motor unit consists of a single motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it innervates. When the motor neuron fires, all the muscle fibers in its motor unit contract simultaneously. The ratio of muscle fibers to motor neuron varies: eye muscles have small motor units (fine precision), while back muscles have large motor units (high force). The nervous system recruits motor units in order, using small units first for delicate tasks and progressively larger units for heavy loads."

**Source:** Custom diagram. Ensure visual distinction between the neuron (single, branching) and the muscle fibers (multiple, receiving branches).

## IMAGE 8: Tissue Integration in the Intestinal Wall (Optional, for Integration Section)

**Placement:** "Integration: How Tissues Build an Organ" section.

**Size & Format:** Cross-section diagram of intestinal wall showing all four layers. ~12 cm wide.

**What it shows:**
- **Layer 1 (Apical):** Epithelium (simple columnar with microvilli). Label: epithelium, microvilli, goblet cells.
- **Layer 2:** Lamina propria (loose connective tissue with blood vessels and nerves). Label: lamina propria, blood vessel, nerve.
- **Layer 3:** Submucosa (connective tissue with glands). Label: submucosa, gland.
- **Layer 4 (Deepest):** Muscularis (smooth muscle). Label: circular muscle, longitudinal muscle.

Each layer should have a different color.

**Pedagogical purpose:**
Students see that an organ is not made of one tissue type. Each tissue is essential. The epithelium absorbs, the connective tissue supports and carries nutrients/nerves, the muscle moves the contents forward.

**Caption:**
"The intestinal wall is a tissue-level integration. The epithelium (innermost) absorbs nutrients and acts as a selective barrier. The lamina propria (connective tissue) provides structural support and carries blood vessels. The submucosa (deeper connective tissue) contains glands. The muscularis (smooth muscle) contracts to move food along the digestive tract. Remove any layer and the intestine loses function. Tissues work together to create organ-level function."

**Source:** Custom diagram or OpenStax digestive system figure adapted for tissue-level annotation.

## IMAGE SOURCING NOTES

**Primary source:** OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, Chapter 4 (Tissue Level of Organization). This textbook has high-quality histological images.

**Secondary sources if needed:**
- BioRender (for custom diagrams of junctions, neuron structure, motor units)
- Wikimedia Commons (histology images, public domain)
- Khan Academy anatomy resources

**Magnification standards:**
- Epithelial cell types: Light microscopy, ~400x magnification (cells visible but not subcellular detail)
- Connective tissues: Light microscopy, ~200–400x (fiber structure visible, cells visible)
- Muscle tissues: Light microscopy, ~400x (striations visible, cell boundaries clear)
- Neuron: Diagram or fluorescence microscopy, not light microscopy (neurons are thin and need special staining or immunofluorescence)
- Motor unit: Diagram (actual images would require electron microscopy or special histological prep)

**Accessibility:**
- Color-blind friendly color schemes (avoid red-green distinction as the only visual cue)
- High contrast between structures and background
- Clear labels in sans-serif font (Arial, Helvetica, or equivalent)
- Captions that explain what the student should see, not just what the figure shows
