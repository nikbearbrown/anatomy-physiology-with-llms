## Figure 1: The Alimentary Canal—Regions and Epithelial Types

[FIGURE: Diagram showing longitudinal cross-section of digestive tract from mouth to anus]

Labels:
- Mouth: stratified squamous epithelium
- Pharynx: stratified squamous epithelium
- Esophagus: stratified squamous epithelium
- Stomach: simple columnar epithelium with gastric glands
- Duodenum: simple columnar epithelium with villi
- Jejunum and Ileum: simple columnar epithelium with prominent villi
- Colon: simple columnar epithelium, no villi
- Rectum and Anus: simple columnar to stratified squamous

Key point: The tube changes its epithelial type and structure at each transition. The stomach appears as a sac; the small intestine is folded.

## Figure 2: The Small Intestine—Surface Area Amplification

[FIGURE: Series of progressively zoomed views]

Panel A: Gross anatomy
- The small intestine appears as a coiled tube ~3 meters long

Panel B: Mucosal folds (plicae circulares)
- The epithelium is folded like an accordion
- 10-fold increase in surface area from simple tube

Panel C: Villi
- Finger-like projections extending into lumen
- Each villus 0.5-1 mm long
- Contain: epithelial cells, blood vessels (for nutrient transport), lacteals (for fat transport)
- 10-fold additional increase

Panel D: Microvilli (electron microscopy view)
- Projections on apical surface of epithelial cells
- ~1,000 per cell
- Each ~1 micrometer long
- Create "brush border"
- 10-20 fold additional increase

Total amplification: ~2,400 cm² (simple tube) → 200-400 m² (with all amplifications)

## Figure 3: Layers of the Alimentary Canal

[FIGURE: Histological cross-section of small intestine wall]

From lumen outward:
- **Mucosa** (innermost)
  - Epithelium (simple columnar)
  - Lamina propria (connective tissue with blood vessels, lymphoid tissue, nerve endings)
  - Muscularis mucosae (thin smooth muscle layer)

- **Submucosa**
  - Dense connective tissue
  - Blood and lymphatic vessels
  - Submucosal plexus (nerve network)

- **Muscularis** (muscle layer)
  - Circular smooth muscle (inner)
  - Longitudinal smooth muscle (outer)
  - Myenteric plexus (nerve network between layers)

- **Serosa** (outermost, in abdominal cavity)
  - Visceral peritoneum
  - Loose connective tissue

## Figure 5: The Stomach—Structure and Churning

[FIGURE: Anatomical diagram of stomach with detail of wall layers]

Panel A: Gross anatomy
- Cardia (entry from esophagus)
- Fundus (upper chamber)
- Body (main chamber)
- Pylorus (exit valve to small intestine)
- Shows three-layer muscularis: oblique (innermost), circular, longitudinal (outermost)

Panel B: Gastric glands in wall
- Parietal cells: secrete HCl, intrinsic factor
- Chief cells: secrete pepsinogen
- Mucus cells: secrete protective mucus
- Enteroendocrine cells: secrete gastrin

Panel C: Churning pattern
- Circular arrows show rotational mixing
- Oblique muscle layer allows churning in multiple directions
- Three-dimensional mixing more effective than simple propulsion

Key point: The stomach's job is to churn, not just push. The three-muscle-layer arrangement enables this.

## Figure 6: Accessory Organs—Liver, Pancreas, Gallbladder

[FIGURE: Anatomical relationship of liver, pancreas, gallbladder, and duodenum]

Labels:
- Liver: sits under right rib cage, produces bile continuously
- Gallbladder: small sac tucked under liver edge, stores and concentrates bile
- Hepatic duct: carries bile from liver
- Cystic duct: carries bile from gallbladder
- Common bile duct: combines hepatic and cystic ducts, enters duodenum
- Pancreas: behind stomach, extends toward duodenum
- Pancreatic duct: carries enzymes and bicarbonate to duodenum

Arrows show: bile flow (liver → gallbladder, gallbladder → duodenum), enzyme/bicarbonate flow (pancreas → duodenum)

Key point: These three organs deliver their secretions at precise times and concentrations via hormone signals.

## Figure 7: Nutrient Absorption Mechanisms

[FIGURE: Cross-section of epithelial cell showing different transport pathways]

Panel A: Glucose and amino acids (active transport)
- SGLT1 cotransporter at apical membrane (facing lumen)
- Glucose enters with Na+ (cotransport)
- Na+/K+ ATPase at basolateral membrane pumps Na+ out using ATP
- Gradient created drives cotransporter
- GLUT2 transporter at basolateral membrane exports glucose to blood

Panel B: Fats (micellar solubilization)
- Bile salts (in yellow) form mixed micelles with monoglycerides and fatty acids
- Mixed micelle approaches epithelial cell
- Fatty acid/monoglyceride released at brush border
- Absorbed into cell
- Resynthesized into triglycerides
- Packaged into chylomicron with cholesterol and protein
- Chylomicron exits via lactal to lymphatic system

Panel C: Water-soluble vitamins (transporters)
- Specific transporter proteins for each vitamin type
- Transport across apical and basolateral membranes
- Energy requirement varies (some active, some passive)

Key point: Different nutrients take different paths based on their chemical properties.

## Figure 8: Bile Salt Emulsification

[FIGURE: Molecular diagram showing fat droplet emulsification]

Panel A: Without bile salts
- Large fat globule (hydrophobic)
- Poorly accessible to lipase enzymes
- Mostly aqueous environment around droplet

Panel B: With bile salts
- Bile salt molecules surround fat droplet
- Hydrophobic side of bile salt faces fat
- Hydrophilic side faces water
- Large droplet broken into many small droplets
- Much greater surface area exposed to lipase

Key: This is NOT chemical digestion (bonds not broken). It is physical division that increases enzyme access.

## Figure 9: Hormonal Control of Digestion

[FIGURE: Timeline showing hormone secretion and effects]

Time axis: 0 (food enters stomach) to 5 hours (end of small intestine)

**Stomach phase (0-2 hours)**
- Food detected in stomach
- G cells secrete gastrin
- Gastrin stimulates parietal cells → HCl secretion

**Small intestine phase (2-5 hours)**
- Acidic chyme enters duodenum
- S cells secrete secretin → pancreas releases bicarbonate (pH rises)
- I cells secrete CCK → gallbladder contracts (bile released), pancreas releases enzymes
- K cells secrete GIP → slows gastric emptying (prevents too-rapid entry)

Arrows show: hormone release, target organ, effect

Key point: Local chemical signals, not central brain commands, orchestrate the sequence.

## Figure 10: The Enteric Nervous System

[FIGURE: Schematic of nerve plexuses in alimentary canal wall]

Panel A: Cross-section of intestinal wall showing two nerve plexuses
- Myenteric plexus (plexus of Auerbach): between circular and longitudinal muscle layers
  - Controls: motility (peristalsis, segmentation)
  - Contains: ~100 million neurons
  
- Submucosal plexus (plexus of Meissner): in submucosa
  - Controls: secretion, sensory input
  - Smaller than myenteric plexus

Panel B: Connectivity
- Enteric plexuses communicate with each other
- Receive extrinsic input from autonomic nervous system (sympathetic, parasympathetic)
- Can function semi-autonomously (don't need brain input for basic digestion)

Key point: The gut has its own nervous system, sometimes called the "second brain."

## Figure 11: The Pressure-Volume Relationship—Pylorus and Stomach Emptying

[FIGURE: Graph showing how pylorus controls emptying rate]

X-axis: Time (hours)
Y-axis: Stomach volume (mL) and chyme entering duodenum (mL/min)

Panel A: Stomach volume decreases over time
- Starts: ~1,500 mL (full)
- 2 hours: ~500 mL
- 4 hours: ~100-200 mL
- 5+ hours: nearly empty

Curve is NOT linear—slopes change based on meal composition.

Panel B: Pylorus opening (chyme flow)
- Remains relatively constant: ~15-30 mL per 30 seconds
- Rate controlled by: CCK (slows if high fat content), GIP (slows if high glucose content)
- Regulated to prevent duodenum from being overwhelmed

Key point: The pylorus is a "intelligent valve" that matches emptying rate to the intestine's ability to process.

Key point: The small intestine does most of the work, with jejunum being the major absorption site.

## Figure 13: Water Balance in the Gut

[FIGURE: Flow diagram showing water input and absorption]

Inputs:
- Drinking: ~2 liters
- Saliva: ~1.5 liters
- Gastric juice: ~2.5 liters
- Bile: ~1 liter
- Pancreatic juice: ~2.5 liters
- Intestinal juice: ~3 liters
- **Total: ~10 liters/day**

Outputs:
- Absorbed in small intestine: ~8 liters
- Absorbed in colon: ~2 liters
- **Total absorbed: ~10 liters**

Excreted in feces: ~100-200 mL (normal)

Key point: The digestive system secretes nearly 10 liters of fluid daily. Nearly all is reabsorbed, leaving only a small amount in feces.

## Figure 14: Microbiota and Fermentation

[FIGURE: Schematic of colon showing bacterial fermentation]

Panel A: Bacterial species
- Shows schematic of colon lumen
- Bacteria density increases from duodenum (few) to colon (trillions)
- Major groups: Bacteroides, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria

Panel B: Fermentation pathway
- Dietary fiber (indigestible carbohydrate) enters colon
- Bacteria ferment using anaerobic metabolism
- Products: short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, propionate, acetate)
- Bacterial synthesis: B vitamins, vitamin K
- Colonic epithelium absorbs fatty acids and vitamins

Panel C: Health implications
- Normal flora: protects against pathogens, provides vitamins
- Disrupted flora (after antibiotics): vulnerability to *C. difficile* overgrowth
- Diversity important: low diversity associated with disease
