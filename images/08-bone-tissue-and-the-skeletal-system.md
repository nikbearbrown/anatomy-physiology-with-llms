# Bone Tissue and the Skeletal System — Images and Figures

## Figure Placements and Descriptions

### FIGURE 1: Anatomy of a Long Bone (Concept 1 — gross anatomy section)

**Placement:** After the worked example "Why a bone does not snap like a chalk stick." This figure should show the anatomy being described in the text.

**Content:** A longitudinal cross-section of the femur showing:
- The diaphysis (shaft) labeled, with compact bone walls
- The medullary cavity (hollow center) labeled, with yellow marrow indicated
- The periosteum labeled on the outer surface
- The epiphyses (both ends) labeled, filled with spongy bone
- Red marrow indicated in spongy regions
- The metaphysis (transition zone) labeled
- Articular cartilage at the very end
- A nutrient foramen shown where a blood vessel enters

**Pedagogical purpose:** This grounds the reader's understanding of the gross anatomy. The figure makes the three-dimensional structure visible and shows why the hollow design is efficient. The periosteum, endosteum, and marrow locations become concrete rather than abstract.

**Style notes:** Keep it clean. Label clearly. Show the contrast between the dense compact bone of the shaft and the open lattice of spongy bone at the ends.

### FIGURE 2: Compact Bone Microscopy—The Osteon (Concept 1 — end of section)

**Placement:** After the text describes osteons, lacunae, and canaliculi. This moves the reader from gross to microscopic scale.

**Content:** A microscopic view of a cross-section of compact bone showing:
- A central canal (Haversian canal) with blood vessels
- Concentric rings of lamellae around the canal
- Lacunae (small spaces) at the borders of lamellae
- Osteocytes within lacunae
- Canaliculi radiating from lacunae, connecting to adjacent lacunae and to the central canal
- Perforating canals (Volkmann's canals) branching off at right angles

**Pedagogical purpose:** This shows students the microscopic architecture of bone and helps them see how osteocytes are positioned to sense stress and communicate. The canaliculi are the key feature—they show that osteocytes are not isolated but networked.

**Style notes:** This should look almost like a cross-section of tree rings (the lamellae) with small cellular outposts (lacunae) at regular intervals. The canaliculi should look like a communication network.

---

### FIGURE 3: Spongy Bone Architecture (Concept 1 — end of section)

**Placement:** Immediately after or alongside Figure 2, to contrast with the dense rings of compact bone.

**Content:** A microscopic view of spongy bone showing:
- Trabeculae (thin struts of bone) arranged in a lattice
- The trabeculae aligned along lines of stress (not random)
- Spaces between trabeculae (not shown filled, to illustrate the openness)
- Osteocytes in lacunae within the trabeculae
- Indication of marrow in the spaces between trabeculae

**Pedagogical purpose:** This illustrates the principle of stress-aligned architecture. Students should see that the trabeculae are *not* random but follow lines of mechanical stress. This reinforces the concept that bone adapts to load.

**Style notes:** Show a few trabeculae in detail and their spatial arrangement. The lattice should clearly show that this structure is much lighter than solid bone while still being strong.

### FIGURE 4: The Four Bone Cell Types and Their Locations (Concept 2 — cell types section)

**Placement:** Early in Concept 2, after introducing osteogenic cells, osteoblasts, osteocytes, and osteoclasts.

**Content:** A schematic diagram showing:
- Osteogenic cells in the deep periosteum (undifferentiated, ready to divide)
- Osteoblasts actively laying down matrix at a bone surface (with lacunae forming behind them)
- Osteocytes in lacunae within mature bone, reaching out through canaliculi
- Osteoclasts on a bone surface, actively resorbing (shown as large, multinucleated cells)
- A timeline or arrow showing the transformation: osteogenic → osteoblast → osteocyte

**Pedagogical purpose:** This helps students understand the cell types, their spatial locations, and their relationships. The transformation sequence is crucial—students often think these are four separate lineages rather than a developmental progression (with osteoclasts being the exception).

**Style notes:** Color code the cell types (e.g., osteogenic = orange, osteoblast = yellow, osteocyte = green, osteoclast = red). Show the periosteum and endosteum as contexts where these cells operate.

### FIGURE 5: The Bone Remodeling Cycle (Concept 2 — remodeling section)

**Placement:** After the worked example "Why immobilization weakens bone."

**Content:** A timeline or cyclical diagram showing:
- Stage 1: Osteoclasts arriving and resorbing bone (weeks 1-4)
- Stage 2: Osteoclasts leaving, osteoblasts arriving (week 4)
- Stage 3: Osteoblasts laying down new bone (weeks 4-12)
- Stage 4: Bone fully reformed, ready for the next cycle
- Timeline label: "Complete remodeling cycle: 7-10 years"

**Pedagogical purpose:** This illustrates the remodeling timeline and helps students understand why bone loss can happen quickly (osteoclasts work fast) but bone gain happens slowly (osteoblasts work over months).

**Style notes:** Use a circular diagram to emphasize the cyclical nature. Show the approximate timescales. Indicate how factors like immobilization or estrogen deficiency disrupt this cycle.

### FIGURE 6: Calcium Homeostasis—PTH and Calcitonin Feedback (Concept 3 — hormonal regulation section)

**Placement:** After the explanation of PTH and calcitonin mechanisms.

**Content:** A flow diagram showing:
- Parathyroid glands detecting low blood calcium
- PTH release
- PTH effects on three targets: bone (osteoclast activation → calcium release), kidney (calcium reabsorption), intestine (calcitriol production → calcium absorption)
- Blood calcium rising back to normal
- Thyroid gland detecting high calcium
- Calcitonin release
- Calcitonin effects: osteoclast inhibition, osteoblast stimulation, kidney calcium loss
- Blood calcium returning to normal

**Pedagogical purpose:** This shows the dual-hormone system maintaining homeostasis and helps students understand negative feedback. The diagram should make clear that PTH and calcitonin work in opposite directions.

**Style notes:** Use separate colors for PTH (blue?) and calcitonin (red?) pathways. Show the endpoints (bone, kidney, intestine) clearly. Include the normal blood calcium range (10 mg/dL).

### FIGURE 7: Fracture Repair Stages (Integration or exercises section)

**Placement:** Not required in the main chapter narrative (since fractures are not a primary concept focus), but could be included in the exercises section when working through example problems.

**Content:** Four sequential images showing:
- Stage 1: Hematoma (blood clot) at fracture site
- Stage 2: Internal callus (fibrocartilaginous matrix from endosteum) and external callus (bone and cartilage from periosteum)
- Stage 3: Cartilage replaced by trabecular bone
- Stage 4: Remodeling complete, normal bone contours restored

**Pedagogical purpose:** This supports the integration section's discussion of how the skeleton repairs itself and reinforces the concept of staged, orchestrated cell behavior.

**Style notes:** Show the progression clearly. Label the calluses and the tissues involved in each stage.

### FIGURE 8: Effect of Immobilization on Bone Density (Optional — for exercises)

**Placement:** Could be included as supplementary material for the immobilization worked example.

**Content:** A comparison of:
- An active leg showing normal or increasing bone density
- An immobilized leg showing rapid loss of bone density
- Timeline showing the divergence over 6 weeks
- Recovery timeline showing the immobilized leg rebuilding bone slowly after cast removal

**Pedagogical purpose:** This visually demonstrates the principle that osteocytes need stress signals to maintain bone, and how quickly bone is lost without those signals.

**Style notes:** Could be a simple line graph showing bone density over time for both legs, or a visual representation of cross-sections before, during, and after immobilization.

### FIGURE 9: Postmenopausal Bone Loss (Optional — for exercises)

**Placement:** Could be included as supplementary material for the postmenopausal woman example.

**Content:** A graph or bar chart showing:
- Bone density at age 20, 30 (peak), 40, 50 (menopause), 60, 70
- A marked acceleration of loss around age 50
- Comparison with a male trajectory (less dramatic loss)
- Threshold for osteoporosis marked

**Pedagogical purpose:** This illustrates how hormonal changes (estrogen) affect bone density over the lifespan and emphasizes the vulnerability of women in their 50s-60s.

**Style notes:** Show the natural age-related loss as a gentle slope, and the menopausal acceleration as a steeper drop. Label the fracture risk threshold.

## General Style Notes for All Figures

1. **Clarity over realism:** These are teaching figures. They should be clear, labeled, and appropriate to the level of detail in the text. A simplified schematic is often better than a photorealistic image.

2. **Color coding:** Consistent color coding across figures (e.g., osteoblasts always yellow, osteoclasts always red) helps students build mental models.

3. **Scale awareness:** When appropriate, indicate the scale (gross anatomy, microscopic, molecular). Figures 1-3 progress in magnification and should make this clear.

4. **Label placement:** Labels should be placed clearly, with lines or pointers if needed. Avoid cluttering.

5. **Attenborough × Feynman aesthetics:** Figures should support the narrative-explanatory voice. They should show how things work, not just what they look like. Include process and causality where possible (e.g., Figure 6 should show cause-and-effect relationships, not just anatomy).

6. **Figure captions:** Each caption should answer: "What is this figure showing?" and "Why should the reader care?" Example: "The osteon's concentric lamellae and central canal illustrate how blood vessels are distributed throughout compact bone, allowing nutrients to reach osteocytes deep within the matrix."
