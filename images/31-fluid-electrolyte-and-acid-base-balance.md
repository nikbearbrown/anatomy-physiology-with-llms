### Concept 2: Electrolytes and Ion Balance

**[FIGURE: Ion concentration differences between ICF and ECF]**
- Bar chart comparing Na+, K+, Cl-, HCO3-, Ca++, and HPO4 2- in plasma vs. ICF
- Clearly shows Na+/Cl- high in ECF; K+/HPO4 2- high in ICF
- Annotations noting the role of the sodium-potassium pump in maintaining these gradients
- Source: Standard ion distribution chart from OpenStax CNX

**[FIGURE: The sodium-potassium pump mechanism]**
- Step-by-step molecular diagram showing:
  1. Na+ binding to pump protein inside cell
  2. ATP hydrolysis and conformational change
  3. Na+ release outside; K+ binding outside
  4. K+ brought back in; pump returns to resting state
- Shows that 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in per cycle
- Indicates net electrogenic effect (1 positive charge leaves per cycle)
- Source: Molecular machinery of active transport

**[FIGURE: Capillary exchange and edema formation]**
- Left side: normal capillary with balanced hydrostatic and osmotic pressures
  - Arteriolar end: hydrostatic pressure > osmotic pressure → fluid leaves
  - Venous end: hydrostatic pressure < osmotic pressure → fluid enters
- Right side: pathologic capillary (in edema)
  - Shows either elevated hydrostatic pressure (heart failure, venous obstruction) or decreased osmotic pressure (low plasma protein in liver disease)
  - Fluid accumulation in interstitial space
- Source: Starling forces and capillary exchange illustration

**[FIGURE: Normal serum electrolyte ranges]**

- Color-coded zones: normal (green), low (blue), high (red)

### Concept 3: Acid-Base Balance

**[FIGURE: The pH scale and physiologic pH]**
- Linear pH scale from 0 to 14
- Mark the blood pH window (7.35–7.45) as a narrow band
- Show examples at each end: acidic (stomach acid pH 2, lemon juice pH 2.5) and basic (sodium hydroxide pH 13)
- Emphasis on how narrow the physiologic range is relative to the scale
- Source: Standard pH scale illustration

**[FIGURE: The bicarbonate buffer system mechanism]**
- Diagram showing the bidirectional reaction:
  - HCO3- + H+ ↔ H2CO3 ↔ CO2 + H2O
- Left panel: strong acid (HCl) entering blood; HCO3- captures H+; system shifts left
- Right panel: strong base (NaOH) entering blood; H2CO3 donates H+; system shifts right
- Shows that the buffer prevents large pH swings
- Source: Chemical buffer mechanism

**[FIGURE: The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation and the 20:1 ratio]**
- Graphic representation of how pH depends on the ratio of HCO3- to CO2 (or H2CO3)
- Show the 20:1 ratio at normal pH (7.40)
- Demonstrate what happens when ratio changes (e.g., 10:1, 30:1)
- Emphasize that absolute amounts matter less than the ratio
- Source: Quantitative acid-base physiology

**[FIGURE: Respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis]**
- Left: metabolic acidosis develops; HCO3- is consumed by metabolic acid
- Middle: chemoreceptors detect low PCO2 signal
- Right: respiratory rate increases; CO2 is blown off; PCO2 drops; pH is partially restored
- Timeline showing response (minutes)
- Source: Respiratory-metabolic compensation pathway

**[FIGURE: The four acid-base disturbances]**
- 2×2 matrix:
  - Acidosis vs. alkalosis (rows)
  - Metabolic vs. respiratory (columns)
- Each quadrant shows:
  - pH direction (↓ or ↑)
  - Primary abnormality (which of HCO3- or PCO2)
  - Compensation response
  - Common causes
- Color-coded for quick reference
- Source: Clinical acid-base classification

**[FIGURE: Blood gas interpretation flowchart]**
- Decision tree for interpreting ABG values:
  1. Determine if pH is low, normal, or high
  2. Determine which of HCO3- or PCO2 is abnormal in same direction as pH
  3. Assess whether compensation is appropriate
  4. Diagnose primary disorder
- Example paths for each of the four disturbances
- Source: Clinical diagnostic tool

## Supporting Illustrations

**[FIGURE: The kidney and bicarbonate reabsorption]**
- Diagram of proximal tubule cell and collecting duct cell
- Shows the cycle:
  1. H+ is secreted into tubular fluid
  2. H+ combines with HCO3- to form H2CO3
  3. Carbonic anhydrase converts H2CO3 to CO2 + H2O
  4. CO2 diffuses into the cell
  5. Inside cell, CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 → HCO3- + H+
  6. HCO3- exits into blood; H+ is secreted again
- Emphasizes the conservation rather than excretion of bicarbonate
- Source: Renal acid-base physiology

**[FIGURE: Chronic respiratory acidosis and renal compensation]**
- Graph showing how pH and HCO3- change over time in emphysema:
  - Acute: PCO2 rises, pH drops, HCO3- is normal initially
  - Over days: kidneys increase bicarbonate reabsorption
  - Chronic: pH comes back toward normal but HCO3- is elevated
- Shows the concept of "compensated respiratory acidosis"
- Source: Time-dependent compensation in chronic lung disease

**[FIGURE: Hypokalemia and cardiac arrhythmia]**
- EKG tracings comparing normal and hypokalemic patterns
- Shows: flattened T wave, prolonged QT interval, increased risk of arrhythmia
- Hyperkalemia EKG: peaked T wave, shortened PR interval, wide QRS
- Annotations explaining why the changes occur (altered cardiac action potential)
- Source: Electrophysiology and clinical EKG correlation
